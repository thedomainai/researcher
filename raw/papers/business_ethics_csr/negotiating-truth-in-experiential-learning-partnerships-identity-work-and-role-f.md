# Negotiating Truth in Experiential Learning Partnerships: Identity Work and Role Flexing in Higher Education-Business Collaborations

**著者**: Stacey Bushfield, Sarah Sholl
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 経営倫理・CSR

## Abstract

The notion of ‘truth’ within organizational and educational contexts is increasingly contested. Scholars and practitioners alike struggle with competing narratives about what constitutes credible knowledge, authentic learning, and legitimate expertise. Higher Education (HE) institutions and the Business School’s within them have traditionally positioned themselves as sources of expertise and truth, with research creating knowledge that precedes and guides practical action. However, as Chia and Holt (2008) observed almost two decades ago, there are doubts as to the legitimacy of this given the dynamic nature of organisational life and the reality that business decisions are shaped by unpredictable emotions that influence how managers frame choices. The emergence of generative artificial intelligence (AI) has further deepened the divide between the ‘truth’ of academic theory and the ‘truth’ of practical application. Managers now have access to knowledge at the finger tips and can implement almost immediately, but from a critical research perspective this raises concerns surrounding the biases inherent within AI systems and depth of analysis informing decisions (Montealegre-López, 2025). Against this backdrop, experiential learning (EL) partnerships, where universities collaborate with industry to deliver work-integrated projects, offer a compelling lens through which to examine how ‘truths’ about competence, professionalism, and partnership legitimacy are constructed, negotiated, and contested.Experiential learning modifies traditional pedagogical structures, requiring students, academics, and business partners to engage in new forms of collaboration and identity work. These triadic partnerships involve shifting role expectations and hybrid identities, creating spaces where participants must reconcile multiple, sometimes conflicting, versions of reality (Amigó & Lloyd, 2021; Basit et al., 2015). Our recent systematic review of thirty-nine empirical studies highlights persistent tensions in defining mentorship roles and managing identity transitions in EL contexts, revealing gaps in understanding how these dynamics shape perceptions of authenticity and trust. Moreover, emotional investments (including, for example, fear of failure, hope for employability, and the desire for belonging) play a critical role in sustaining or challenging these negotiated truths, yet remain underexplored in current literature.This developmental proposal builds on these insights and an ongoing empirical study funded by the Royal Society of Edinburgh. It seeks to investigate how stakeholders in EL partnerships negotiate truths about roles, identities, and learning outcomes, and how these processes are influenced by emotional dynamics and emerging technologies. By situating experiential learning within the broader discourse on truth-making, this research aims to contribute to theoretical debates on identity and legitimacy while offering practical strategies for managing contested truths in higher education-business collaborations.
