# AI Urbanism

**著者**: Miguel Valdez
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 社会学

## Abstract

Senior Lecturer, School of Engineering and Innovation AI urbanism is an endeavor and field of study informed, inter alia, by urban geography, computer science, innovation studies, human-computer interaction and urban planning. This interdisciplinary nature is reflected in a pragmatical body of literature concerned with actually existing practices, regulations, and institutions to improve urban life and urban planning, counterbalanced by critical inquiries of the role of AI in the development of cities. A common thread running across AI urbanism is the belief that innovation in artificial intelligence will transform cities in unprecedented ways. AI has reinvigorated longstanding debates about how technical objects may extend the agency of their creators or alternatively become lively objects displaying emergent behaviors unforeseen by their designers. Debates on the co-constitution of humans, technologies, and cities introduced in Bruno Latour’s Actor Network Theory, Donna Haraway’s Cyborg Theory, or Gillian Rose’s work on post-human agencies in the smart city gain renewed urgency as, for the first time in history, control of the city is not determined exclusively by human intelligences. Here, “intelligences” is pluralized to emphasize that AI is not a unique, clearly defined entity but a broad label encompassing multiple forms of intelligence, some already existing and some occupying a liminal space that intersects science fiction, techno-boosterism, speculation, and insightful anticipation. AI urbanism is thus concerned with the urban application of multiple intelligences, from advanced (and currently imaginary) general intelligences with capabilities exceeding those of humans, to already widespread AI applications attuned to narrow, specific tasks. For purposes of this article, AI will be organized in terms of a broad taxonomy comprising autonomous vehicles (AVs) and mobilities, urban robots and drones, and less tangible manifestations such as city brains, digital twins, and urban platforms which cannot be readily located in space but impactfully transform our ways of knowing, experiencing, and acting upon cities. In addition to an introduction and three sections attending to the categories of urban AI outlined above, three additional sections address issues of relevance to all applications. The fifth section attends to the challenges of planning for AI (and with AI). The sixth section engages with a body of literature produced during a pandemic which, perhaps not coincidentally, preceded the rapid development of urban AI and drew attention to the role of artificial intelligences in a world in crisis. The final section, dedicated to AI un urban and peri-urban agriculture, draws attention to the potential of AI to extend not only the definition of urban but also the ways we conceive the relation between humans, machines, and nature.
