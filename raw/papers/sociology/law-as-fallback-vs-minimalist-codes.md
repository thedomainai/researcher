# Law as Fallback vs Minimalist Codes

**著者**: Daniel Rosehill, Gemini 3.1 (Flash), Chatterbox TTS
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 社会学

## Abstract

Episode summary: What happens when a country writes thousands of laws but almost never enforces them? This episode contrasts Japan's granular fallback model — where dense legal codes function as social furniture — against minimalist systems like the US Constitution, where brevity shifts complexity to the courts. We explore how Singapore actively enforces its detailed laws, why minimalism can be more expensive than granularity, and what the EU AI Act reveals about these tradeoffs in real time. A legal philosophy twofer with implications for AI regulation, trade policy, and platform governance. Show Notes This episode compares two opposing styles of jurisprudence: granular legal codes that function as social fallbacks versus minimalist codes that leave most behavior unregulated. In Japan, dense laws like the 1947 Local Autonomy Law (over 260 articles) are rarely litigated — fewer than 5% of articles have seen contested cases in 80 years. Instead, laws provide the architecture for administrative guidance, where a bureaucrat's phone call carries social weight because formal enforcement looms in the background. This works in high-trust societies but becomes fragile when norms fracture, leaving a massive code that nobody knows how to enforce. Minimalist systems like the US Constitution shift legal complexity from legislation to adjudication. The Commerce Clause's 14 words generated over 1,000 Supreme Court interpretations since 1824. The US spends 2.3% of GDP on legal services versus Japan's 0.8% — minimalism is actually more expensive. Regulatory gaps become policy choices by silence, as seen in Sackett v. EPA, where narrowing the Clean Water Act's definition left millions of wetland acres unprotected. Singapore offers a third path: granular codes actively enforced from day one, treating law as operating instructions rather than social furniture. These tradeoffs are playing out now in AI regulation, where the EU's hyper-granular AI Act (113 articles, 180+ pages) contrasts with America's wait-and-see approach.
