# World Systems Engineering Organization, Systems, Coordination, and the Architecture of Civilizational Futures A Thirty-Axis Research Architecture for Polycentric Organization, Interdependent Systems, Coordination, Resilience, Long-Term Vision, and Intergenerational Handoff

**著者**: 政恩 馮
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: システム工学

## Abstract

Description World Systems Engineering develops a civilization-scale research architecture for understanding how an increasingly interdependent world can organize, coordinate, adapt, recover from failure, and preserve meaningful future options without collapsing into either fragmentation or coercive uniformity. Its central question is: How can a world organize itself well enough to coordinate, remain diverse enough to learn, become resilient enough to survive failure, and stay open enough for future generations to begin again? The framework begins from a simple observation: Many of the most important civilizational failures are not failures of individual components. They are failures of relationships among components. Energy affects food and water. Digital infrastructure affects finance, health, communication, and governance. Artificial intelligence changes the speed and scale of coordination. Economic decisions interact with ecological constraints. Institutional choices can impose consequences on people and generations who were never represented when those choices were made. World Systems Engineering therefore shifts attention from isolated institutions toward: interfaces,dependencies,feedback loops,coordination capacity,distributional effects,failure propagation,correctability,and intergenerational options. The framework begins where peace engineering ends. Preventing war is essential, but peace alone does not guarantee a mature civilization. A world may be peaceful while remaining brittle, unequal, ecologically unstable, technologically dependent, institutionally opaque, or incapable of adapting to rapid change. The constructive question is therefore: If civilization succeeds in avoiding destruction, how should it organize the conditions of shared life? The framework proposes six foundational structures. 1. Organization Architecture Organization answers: Who decides?Who is responsible?At what scale should authority operate? This structure examines nested governance, subsidiarity, polycentric authority, institutional interfaces, responsibility chains, legitimacy, and representation. The goal is not maximum centralization. It is appropriate distribution of authority. Local problems should remain local when possible. Cross-border and planetary problems require broader coordination when local capacity is insufficient. The preferred architecture is polycentric: multiple centers of decision-making,shared interfaces,visible responsibilities,mutual constraints,and the ability to learn across institutional differences. 2. System Architecture System Architecture examines how essential civilizational functions interact. These include: energy,food,water,digital infrastructure,artificial intelligence,economic and financial systems,public health,and human security. Modern civilization depends on tightly coupled systems. Such coupling creates enormous productive capacity, but it can also create cascade risk. A failure in one infrastructure can propagate into many others. The objective is therefore not simply to optimize individual systems. It is to understand the dependencies among them. A mature system architecture balances: efficiency,redundancy,modularity,interoperability,observability,and repairability. 3. Coordination Architecture A diverse world requires cooperation without requiring ideological uniformity. Coordination Architecture therefore asks: What shared protocols are necessary for different societies, institutions, and intelligent systems to work together? Major research areas include: interoperability,global commons governance,cross-border crisis coordination,multi-agent AI coordination,trust,verification,and information integrity. The framework distinguishes coordination from homogenization. Cooperation does not require everyone to share the same culture, political system, worldview, or model of the good life. It requires enough common structure for interaction to remain understandable and governable. This produces one of the central principles of World Systems Engineering: Coherence ≠ Uniformity and: Integration ≠ Centralization A world can become more coordinated without becoming a single centralized system. 4. Resilience and Correctability No civilization can eliminate uncertainty. No architecture will anticipate every future condition. The more important question is therefore: What happens when the system is wrong? Resilience and Correctability examines: redundancy,modularity,early warning,strategic foresight,shock absorption,continuity,recovery,civilizational repair,rollback,and reversible institutional design. Efficiency alone is not resilience. Systems optimized to remove every redundancy may become cheaper during normal operation while becoming dangerously fragile during crisis. The framework therefore treats failure as an expected design condition. A mature world system should be capable of detecting error, absorbing disruption, maintaining essential functions, learning from failure, and reversing harmful decisions before they become permanently embedded. Correctability is therefore not a secondary feature. It is part of civilizational intelligence. 5. Vision and Future Options Organization and systems can keep civilization operating. They cannot determine what civilization should protect or become. World Systems Engineering therefore includes a dedicated architecture for long-term direction. This structure examines: long-term goal formation,future generations,intergenerational justice,plural futures,cognitive diversity,well-being,flourishing,planetary boundaries,and regenerative development. The framework rejects the assumption that civilization should optimize only for: GDP,speed,technical capability,or short-term output. A mature world must also consider: human agency,health,resilience,ecological stability,distribution,meaning,and future options. Foresight is explicitly distinguished from prediction. Foresight does not claim certainty about what will happen. Its purpose is to preserve options and improve preparedness across multiple plausible futures. 6. Handoff and Re-Origin Civilizations are inherited systems. The people who design institutions today will not control how they are used decades or centuries later. The final structure therefore asks: Can future generations understand what they inherit? Can they inspect it? Can they challenge it? Can they repair it? Can they replace it? And if major inherited systems fail, can civilization begin again? This structure develops concepts including: Civilizational Memory,Version Governance,Successor Autonomy,Re-Origin Capacity,and Open-Future Engineering. The central principle is: Handoff ≠ Obedience A successful civilization does not force future generations to preserve the worldview of its founders. It preserves: knowledge,evidence,failure memory,interfaces,repair capacity,and the freedom to revise inherited systems. Successor autonomy is therefore a design requirement. The framework proposes a heuristic model: World Capacity=Organization× Systems× Coordination× Resilience× Vision× Handoff The multiplicative structure expresses a simple hypothesis: Extreme weakness in one dimension can constrain the performance of the whole civilization. Strong infrastructure without legitimate governance can create domination. Strong organization without functioning systems becomes bureaucracy. Coordination without diversity can become uniformity. Resilience without vision can preserve the wrong things. Vision without handoff can produce founder lock-in. Handoff without memory can reproduce old failures. The work also introduces three additional heuristic models. Systemic Fragility increases with: Coupling× Concentration× Speed× Opacity and is reduced by: Redundancy+ Modularity+ Verification+ Rollback. Open-Future Capacity increases with: Diversity× Reversibility× Learning× Successor Autonomy and decreases with: Lock-In+ Irreversibility+ Dependency. Coordination Adequacy is framed as the relationship between coordination capacity and coordination complexity. These expressions are not proposed as discovered laws of social organization. They are conceptual tools for comparison, stress testing, institutional design, and future research. World Systems Engineering also rejects several common equivalences: Coherence is not uniformity. Integration is not centralization. Capability is not legitimacy. Efficiency is not resilience. Prediction is not foresight. Measurement is not meaning. Safety is not stagnation. Prosperity is not extraction. Handoff is not obedience. Civilization is not finished. The framework is therefore not a blueprint for a single world authority. Its design target is polycentric coherence: enough shared structure to cooperate,enough diversity to learn,enough transparency to verify,enough resilience to survive failure,enough correctability to reverse mistakes,and enough openness for future generations to choose differently. The research architecture is organized across thirty axes and is intended as a seed bank for comparative institutional research, systems modeling, public policy, AI governance, resilience studies, ecological coordination, civilizational design, and intergenerational research. Its deepest proposition is that a mature civilization should not attempt to eliminate complexity. It should learn how to live responsibly within complexity. The ultimate test of a world system is therefore not whether it can completely control the world. It is whether people can remain free and responsible inside systems larger than any single person can understand; whether failures can remain repairable rather than catastrophic; whether cooperation can increase without eliminating difference; and whether future generations inherit more capacity to choose rather than fewer possibilities.
