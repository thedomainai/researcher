# From compliance to resilience: a functional framework for AI-enabled environmental governance

**著者**: Quang-Vinh Dang
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: AIガバナンス

## Abstract

Purpose Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly embedded in corporate environmental governance, yet much of what is labelled AI in this context is difficult to distinguish from conventional rule-based digital automation. Its contribution so far has concentrated on automating compliance reporting rather than building the adaptive capacity that resilience thinking requires. The paper defines what qualifies as AI-enabled environmental governance and develops a framework for distinguishing AI-enabled compliance from AI-enabled resilience, with particular attention to enterprises in middle-income, small and medium enterprise (SME)-dominated economies undergoing simultaneous digital and green transformation. Design/methodology/approach This conceptual paper takes a theory-synthesis approach, combining a technical definition of AI systems with resilience theory, the technology-organization-environment framework and research on greenwashing and AI-washing to propose a four-layer framework: sensing, decision, institutional and equity. Each layer's level of analysis and candidate operational indicators are specified, its relationship to existing digital-maturity models is clarified and its boundary conditions are set out. The central sensing-to-decision mechanism is illustrated with secondary case evidence and examined alongside independent survey-based studies from South and Southeast Asia, whose findings are consistent with, though not a direct test of, the proposed mechanism. Findings The paper argues that AI investment concentrated in the sensing layer, without corresponding investment in the decision and institutional layers, produces compliance-grade rather than resilience-grade outcomes. This pattern is expected to be most pronounced in resource-constrained, SME-dominated settings, where sensing tools can be purchased as point solutions while decision-layer integration requires internal capability that is harder to build. Independent survey evidence from medium enterprises in Indonesia and from the banking sector in India is consistent with this asymmetry, although neither study was designed to test the framework directly. Practical implications The framework offers regulators, firms, financial institutions and educators a vocabulary and a set of candidate indicators for distinguishing genuine resilience investment from compliance automation, useful for designing tiered, capability-aware policy instruments during the current transition towards harmonized international sustainability disclosure standards. Social implications Because environmental shocks fall hardest on those least able to absorb them, the distribution of AI-enabled resilience capability across firms of different sizes is a question of social equity and not only of technical efficiency. The framework's equity layer treats this distribution as a design variable that regulators, financiers and educators can act on, rather than as an unintended side effect of technology diffusion. Originality/value The paper supplies a technical definition of AI-enabled environmental governance and identifies a specific governance failure mode: a layer-specific asymmetry in the cost and purchasability of AI capability that predicts where disclosed adoption and substantive practice are likely to decouple. It proposes candidate indicators for operationalizing the framework's layers at their respective levels of analysis, differentiates this functional approach from adjacent stage-based digital- and environmental, social, and governance (ESG)-maturity models, and narrows its boundary conditions to the institutional settings in which it is expected to apply.
