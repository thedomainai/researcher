# Institutional Readiness before Artificial Intelligence: A Governance Framework for AI-Assisted Judicial Reform in Pakistan

**著者**: Syeda Masheea Fatima
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: AIガバナンス

## Abstract

Courts around the world are facing increasing pressure to improve their performance without compromising impartiality or judicial independence. Artificial intelligence is increasingly being touted as part of the solution. Most governance frameworks concentrate on the responsible use of AI, particularly transparency, accountability and human oversight. Pakistan's National Guidelines for the Use of Artificial Intelligence in Judicial Institutions take a similar approach. What remains less developed is the question of institutional readiness: what should already be working within a court before AI is introduced? Pakistan offers a useful setting in which to examine that question. Judicial reform, digital transformation and AI governance are unfolding at the same time. This overlap makes Pakistan particularly useful for examining whether institutional reform is keeping pace with technological change. This study uses comparative qualitative policy analysis to examine Pakistan's National Guidelines alongside the OECD AI Principles, the UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence and selected international frameworks for judicial AI. The comparison focuses on the institutional assumptions behind these documents, particularly what they expect courts to have in place before their principles can work in practice. The frameworks explain how AI should be used once it has been introduced, yet they offer far less guidance on the institutional arrangements that should already exist before that point. Writing these principles into policy is one thing. Putting them into practice is another. Courts need clear responsibilities, reliable administrative systems and ways of explaining how AI is being used. Without these institutional arrangements, ethical principles risk remaining policy statements instead of changing how the justice system actually works. Despite differences in language and emphasis, similar institutional foundations appeared across almost all of the documents reviewed. Transparent administration, accountable decision-making, human oversight, reliable digital governance and sustained public engagement appear again and again. Together, these features offer a practical way of judging whether a court is actually ready for AI. Looking only at technical capability tells us very little. Technical safeguards such as explainable AI still matter, but they cannot substitute for institutions that lack credibility or public trust. The implication for judicial reform is practical. Before introducing AI, courts should examine whether their existing administrative systems can support transparent, accountable and properly supervised use. Institutional readiness should therefore be assessed before technological readiness. A court may have the technical capacity to use AI without yet having the institutional capacity to govern it well.
