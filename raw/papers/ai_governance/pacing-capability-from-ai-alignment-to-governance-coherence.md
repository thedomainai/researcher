# Pacing Capability: From AI Alignment to Governance Coherence

**著者**: Chris Choi
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: AIガバナンス

## Abstract

Frontier AI has entered a phase in which capability acceleration itself has become a governance variable, not merely a technical one. Traditional AI safety discourse has focused primarily on whether models are aligned with human intentions. Emerging governance concerns increasingly ask whether the rate at which capabilities advance can itself remain governable. Using Dario Amodei's recent proposal for intentionally pacing frontier AI development as a contemporary case, this paper examines the institutional gap between capability and authority. It argues that independent evaluation can improve transparency, legibility, and verification, but that verification alone does not constitute constraint. The deeper governance problem is the conversion of capability into institutional authority through a process of institutional absorption. The paper develops four related distinctions: legibility versus constraint; capability versus authority; vertical versus horizontal pacing; and declared versus verified versus consequential governance. It proposes the analytical sequence: Capability growth → institutional absorption → authority accumulation → governance response. Authority need not be formally delegated. It can accumulate incrementally as recommendations become accepted, workflows adapt, institutional practices form, and reversal becomes increasingly costly. A meaningful pacing regime therefore requires legitimate authority capable of connecting thresholds, evidence, interpretation, and binding consequences, while also addressing systemic effects arising when many institutions absorb similar capabilities simultaneously. The paper further identifies a risk-definition problem: even where institutions possess authority to act, they may disagree about which risk should determine action. Governance coherence therefore requires not only agreement about evidence, but a legitimate basis for resolving disagreement about what the evidence means for permissible action. The paper is diagnostic rather than prescriptive. It does not propose a specific regulatory institution or claim that existing governance models can simply be transferred to AI. Its contribution is to identify the institutional conditions meaningful pacing would require and to distinguish this emerging governance problem from conventional model-level alignment. Central proposition: The governance task is not necessarily to stop capability. It is to ensure that authority does not outrun governance.
