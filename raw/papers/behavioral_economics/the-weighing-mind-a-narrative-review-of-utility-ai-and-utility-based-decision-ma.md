# The Weighing Mind: A Narrative Review of Utility AI and Utility-Based Decision Making in Games, from von Neumann's Theorem to the Behavioral Mathematics

**著者**: Zen Revista, 10 GAME
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 行動経済学

## Abstract

Utility AI is the game agent's weighing mind: the decision architecture—actions scored by their expected utility and the best chosen—whose theory descends from von Neumann and Morgenstern's 1944 expected utility theorem and Kahneman and Tversky's 1979 prospect deviations, and whose game practice—Millington's 2006 scoring systems, Spronck and colleagues' 2006 dynamic scripting, Orkin's 2006 F.E.A.R. planning, Mark's 2009 Behavioral Mathematics for Game AI, Sweetser's 2008 emergence, Bakkes and colleagues' 2009 rapid adaptation, Rabin's 2013 Game AI Pro, and Churchill and Buro's 2013 portfolio search—made the weighing the genre's scalable alternative to the trees. This article presents a narrative review of the primary literature of that architecture. The synthesis is organized around three themes: the decision theory's founding, in which the expected utility's axioms and the prospect's deviations set the theory's frame; the games' practice, in which scoring, adaptation, and planning shipped the weighing; and the architecture's settlement, in which the utility's curves—Mark's behavioral mathematics—made the agent's tradeoffs designable. It is concluded that utility AI's success is the games' economics: continuous scores, emergent priorities, and designable curves—the decision architectures' third paradigm.
