# Embodied AI in the Home: Tradeoffs in Robot Behavior, Autonomy, and User Control

**著者**: Alex Wuqi Zhang
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 行動経済学

## Abstract

A home assistant robot can be useful for the tasks it performs and socially significant for how it participates in everyday life. Combining physical assistance with conversational intelligence brings these roles into the same product, blurring the boundary between a device people use and an agent they interact with. Designing for this emerging category of products requires understanding how choices about a robot's behavior, embodiment, and means of interaction shape both its use and the capabilities people attribute to it. Because these kinds of home assistant robots speak, respond, and act in people's homes, people often come to see them as more than a device. I refer to this as perceived social agency, the sense that a robot acts as an autonomous and socially capable assistant. Preserving this sense of agency matters because it shapes how people respond to a robot. Users are more likely to accept and engage with robots they perceive as socially intelligent and present (Bartneck et al., 2007; De Graaf and Allouch, 2013), and perceiving a robot as a social other rather than a tool is linked to greater trust, enjoyment, and long-term interaction (Breazeal, 2003; De Graaf and Allouch, 2013). In this dissertation, I contribute to this body of work by demonstrating additional social effects of robot interactions with people through a series of user studies. I show that strangers perceived a humanoid robot posing personal conversation questions as more socially present than a poster with the same questions, and discussed the deepest ones more. I also demonstrate that varying a robot's personality during well-being exercises likewise changed users' overall experience and affect. In both cases, my work contributes a more detailed understanding of how perceptions of a robot as a social agent can shape the effects they can have on users. A home assistant robot's behavior also reflects choices about its role in household routines, from how closely it approaches a person to how much conversation it initiates. A default that suits one person may not suit another. A talkative robot may feel engaging to one person and intrusive to another, and the same person may welcome conversation at some times and prefer quiet at others. Customization lets users adapt the robot's behavior to their preferences, which fosters acceptance, satisfaction, and long-term engagement (Broadbent et al., 2009; Irfan et al., 2019; Fronemann et al., 2022). Customization can also involve a tradeoff, however. When users dictate how a robot behaves, its actions may seem like their own instructions replayed, diminishing the very social agency that makes the robot valuable. In this dissertation, I present a series of user studies I have conducted to explore how robots can offer customization while preserving their perceived social agency, through three lenses. First, granularity concerns how much detail users specify. I show how detailed block-based programming takes far longer than broad settings without better matching users' preferences, and lowers the robot's perceived autonomy, social presence, and intelligence. Second, form factor concerns how a robot's appearance shapes these effects. I demonstrate that making a robot's personality customizable lowers its perceived social agency. More strikingly, this customization affects humanoid and non-humanoid robots in very different ways. The decrease in social agency falls largely on the humanoid robot, from which people expect a stable identity, whereas for the non-humanoid robot, customization leaves social agency largely intact and increases rapport. Third, modality concerns how users express preferences, since a visible record may make settings easier to track while speech may better preserve social agency. I show how tablet access brings more confidence and control and lower workload, while speaking to the robot makes it seem more socially agentic. Offering both through a multimodal interface produced the best overall experience while avoiding the lower social agency of tablet-only customization. Together, these findings show that the tradeoff between customization and perceived agency is neither fixed nor uniform. Detailed control, customizing a humanoid robot's personality, and tablet-only customization each diminished the robot's perceived social agency, while broad settings, a non-humanoid form, and speaking to the robot preserved or even strengthened it. By connecting research on end-user customization and on robots as social agents, this dissertation shows that the design decisions behind user-driven customization shape not only what a robot does but how people relate to it. It offers guidance for home robots that people can tailor to their needs while preserving their social presence, an often overlooked part of what makes them valuable.
