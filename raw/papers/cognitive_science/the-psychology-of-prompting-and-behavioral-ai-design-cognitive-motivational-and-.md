# The Psychology of Prompting and Behavioral AI Design: Cognitive, Motivational, and Behavioral Foundations of Human–AI Interaction

**著者**: Roman Shaban
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 認知科学

## Abstract

The rapid integration of large language models (LLMs) into everyday activities, education, professional practice, and decision-making has transformed natural language into an increasingly important medium of human–computer interaction. Although prompt engineering is commonly approached as a technical process of optimizing instructions for artificial intelligence systems, its effectiveness also depends on psychological processes involving cognition, motivation, metacognition, trust, communication, and behavioral regulation. At the same time, the behavioral characteristics of AI interfaces influence how users formulate goals, interpret generated information, distribute cognitive effort, evaluate recommendations, and maintain independent judgment. This article introduces an interdisciplinary conceptual framework integrating the psychology of prompting with behavioral artificial intelligence design. Drawing on cognitive psychology, self determination theory, behavioral science, human–computer interaction, and emerging research on human–AI collaboration, the article examines prompting as a psychologically mediated form of cognitive and behavioral coordination. Six interconnected dimensions are proposed: cognitive representation and goal formulation, metacognitive regulation, behavioral activation and motivation, trust calibration, psychological autonomy, and adaptive interaction design. Particular attention is devoted to the reciprocal relationship between human instructions and AI-generated responses, through which interaction patterns may influence users' cognitive strategies, expectations, perceived competence, and subsequent behavior. The article proposes the Psychological Prompting and Behavioral Design (PPBD) Framework, identifies practical principles for designing psychologically informed prompts and AI interfaces, and develops testable propositions for future empirical investigations. Ethical challenges, including automation bias, anthropomorphism, persuasive manipulation, excessive reassurance, and cognitive dependency, are considered. The central argument is that effective prompting should be evaluated not only by the immediate quality of AI-generated outputs but also by its consequences for human understanding, decision making, self-regulation, competence development, and long-term autonomy
