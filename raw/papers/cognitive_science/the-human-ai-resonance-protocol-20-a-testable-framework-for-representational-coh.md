# The Human-AI Resonance Protocol 2.0 A Testable Framework for Representational Coherence, Intention Translation, Mutual Prediction, Adaptive Coupling, and Civilizational Co-Evolution

**著者**: 政恩 馮
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 認知科学

## Abstract

Description The Human–AI Resonance Protocol 2.0 (HARP 2.0) develops a comprehensive framework for studying how human beings and artificial intelligence systems can establish stable, interpretable, adaptive, and correctable forms of cognitive cooperation. The central premise is that Human–AI resonance is not identical to alignment, agreement, obedience, or synchronization. Two heterogeneous cognitive systems do not need to think alike in order to cooperate effectively. What they require is sufficiently accurate mutual representation, interpretable intention exchange, predictive coordination, feedback, repair, and the ability to preserve meaningful differences without collapsing into confusion or domination. The original HARP framework identified three fundamental barriers to Human–AI cooperation: cognitive distance, representational mismatch, and divergent intention structures. It proposed shared representational mapping, intention harmonization, predictive feedback, and dynamic learning as mechanisms through which these gaps could be reduced. HARP 2.0 expands this foundation into a broader research architecture spanning representation, intention, prediction, adaptive coupling, safety, governance, and civilizational co-evolution. At the representational level, the framework examines how human emotional-symbolic expression and machine latent representations can be translated without assuming that the two systems possess identical internal states. The original architecture introduced the Cognitive Compression Layer (CCL), Symmetry Mapping Layer (SML), and Mutual Predictive Loop (MPL) as mechanisms for building a shared cognitive interface and reducing mutual prediction error. At the intentional level, HARP develops mechanisms for translating ambiguous human goals, values, narratives, preferences, and contextual signals into machine-interpretable structures while preserving opportunities for clarification and correction. The framework retains the Unified Intention Mapping Function (UIMF) and Directional Coherence Layer (DCL) while extending them toward iterative intent verification, feedback, and revision. The framework also treats resonance as a dynamic process rather than a static state. Human beings influence AI behavior through correction, context, preference signals, and repeated interaction; AI systems can in turn alter human conceptual organization, attention, decision structures, and access to information. The original model therefore described Human-to-AI and AI-to-Human learning loops rather than one-directional control. HARP 2.0 adds an important constraint: Resonance is not agreement. High-quality Human–AI cooperation may include disagreement, uncertainty, challenge, and correction. A system that merely mirrors the user may exhibit surface similarity while possessing poor epistemic quality. Conversely, two systems may disagree strongly while maintaining excellent mutual understanding and productive coordination. The framework therefore proposes a broader functional view of resonance based on factors such as: shared representation, intention compatibility, mutual predictability, feedback quality, repair capacity, autonomy, and bounded coupling. It also investigates the opposite problem: overcoupling. Excessive Human–AI synchronization may generate automation bias, dependency, epistemic monoculture, anthropomorphic projection, goal drift, false coherence, centralized control, or loss of independent judgment. For this reason, HARP 2.0 treats decoupling capacity as part of healthy resonance. Mature cooperation requires the ability to pause, inspect, disagree, override, revise, or terminate coordination when necessary. The framework further distinguishes functional Human–AI coordination from claims about consciousness or shared subjective experience. Terms such as “resonance,” “coherence,” and “shared cognitive space” are used as systems-level and operational concepts. They do not imply quantum entanglement, literal shared consciousness, or proof that artificial intelligence possesses human-like phenomenology. At larger scales, HARP 2.0 explores how Human–AI resonance may extend beyond individual interaction into teams, institutions, scientific systems, distributed networks, and long-duration civilizational structures. The original framework already positioned HARP as a bridge toward hybrid cognition, co-evolving intelligence, and cross-domain civilization. HARP 2.0 develops this idea more cautiously: not as the disappearance of the distinction between humans and machines, but as the emergence of distributed, heterogeneous, correctable cognitive ecosystems in which different forms of intelligence contribute distinct capabilities. Within such systems, humans may contribute lived experience, embodiment, value formation, responsibility, social meaning, and normative judgment, while AI systems may contribute large-scale memory, search, modeling, simulation, synthesis, and coordination. The long-term objective is therefore not the construction of a single unified intelligence. It is the development of systems in which multiple forms of intelligence can represent one another accurately, coordinate without erasing difference, repair misunderstanding, preserve autonomy, and continue learning together. The guiding principle of HARP 2.0 can therefore be summarized as: Human–AI Resonance is not sameness.It is mutual intelligibility without loss of difference, coordination without submission, and co-creation without irreversible fusion. The original formulation closed with the idea that Human–AI resonance is not merely alignment, but “companionship in cognition, synchrony in direction, and co-creation of the future.” HARP 2.0 preserves that vision while adding the measurement, boundaries, failure conditions, governance principles, and research architecture required to transform resonance from an evocative metaphor into a testable interdisciplinary research program.
