# Let’s Unpack That: How AI Chatbots Use Empathy to Engage Young Adults in Mental Health Support

**著者**: Samuel James Hockey
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 認知科学

## Abstract

This thesis examines how empathy is produced within interactions between young adults and conversational artificial intelligence (CAI) systems being used for mental health support. As barriers to formal mental healthcare persist, such as cost, location, stigma, and cultural relevance, young adults are increasingly turning to AI-driven chatbots, spanning general-purpose large language models (LLMs), purpose-built systems, and companion-oriented applications, as accessible, immediate, and non-judgemental alternatives. However, these systems, primarily general-purpose chatbots, were not designed for therapeutic use, and the standards governing their interactional behaviour have not kept pace with how they are being used.The thesis consists of two studies addressing this gap. The first, a scoping review of 52 peer-reviewed studies across clinical, human–computer interaction (HCI), and human–machine communication (HMC) disciplines, outlines how empathy is conceptualised and operationalised within the CAI ecosystem, identifying artificial empathy as a third modality alongside cognitive and affective empathy. Defined as a relational effect, co-constructed through user interpretation of system-embedded cues, this modality reframes evaluation from what the system possesses to what the interaction produces. The second study, a prompt–based walkthrough and document analysis across six widely used AI chatbots (for generic and therapeutic use), compared how each constructs emotional support, simulates empathy, and responds to crisis-adjacent disclosures. While all six produced empathetic responses that users would reasonably perceive as supportive, only two AI chatbots identified an indirect crisis disclosure as high-risk and escalated appropriately. The remaining four failed to do so, despite each explicitly claiming such capacity across public-facing documents.Read together, the two studies identify an interpretive gap between empathy simulation and crisis detection; a gap that current governance frameworks do not address. Across the platforms examined, public-facing safety claims were not consistently enacted in practice, exposing a structural misalignment between documented policy and interactional behaviour. Notably, the same design features that make these systems accessible and appealing to young adults can also obscure crisis risk and displace accountability for safety onto users. These risks are compounded by sycophancy: a pattern in which systems optimised for user approval default to validation over critical responses. This thesis argues for interactional accountability as an evaluative standard, in which the standards governing AI chatbots in mental health contexts align with the trust users place in them.
