# Editorial: Revolutionizing obstetric and pediatric pharmacology through artificial intelligence and digital health innovations

**著者**: Jeffrey S. Barrett, Catherine M.T. Sherwin, Samuel L. SewardJr
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: イノベーション管理

## Abstract

Zhang et al. (https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2026.1817282) combined a population pharmacokinetic model of linezolid with machine learning in critically ill children, using SHAP to trace predictions back to clearance, dose, weight, and renal function. Notably, the authors flag their own strong validation metrics as optimistic, since the pharmacokinetic parameters used as model inputs were derived from the same concentration data being predicted, a transparency that usefully clarifies what leakage-free, prospective validation will need to look like before such hybrid models reach the bedside. Westvik et al. (https://doi.org/10.3389/fped.2026.1881474) took a related approach in very preterm infants, estimating serum phosphate levels from routinely collected blood gas, anthropometric, and nutritional data, with small-for-gestational-age status emerging as the dominant risk factor for early hypophosphatemia. Both studies point to the same opportunity that has long motivated modelinformed dosing: extracting more clinical value from data already being collected, rather than adding burden to already vulnerable patients. Recent physiologically based pharmacokinetic modeling of pediatric antibiotic dosing offers a complementary, developmentally grounded route to the same goal (Tanaka et al., 2025).A second cluster of studies extends prediction into real-time obstetric care. Li et al. (https://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2025.1686637) evaluated a consumer smartwatch for detecting spinal anesthesia-induced hypotension during cesarean delivery, finding moderate accuracy at a clinically relevant threshold (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC] 0.87, sensitivity 79.2%, specificity 82.0%) but reduced sensitivity at a milder one, appropriately positioning the device as a screening aid rather than a diagnostic replacement. Wang and He (https://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2026.1794753) developed logistic regression, Naive Bayes, random forest, support vector machine, and XGBoost models to predict intrapartum labor analgesia efficacy from preoperative demographic, clinical, ultrasound, and psychological indicators in 137 parturients; their XGBoost model showed strong discrimination in training (AUC 0.875) that declined in validation (AUC 0.759), an instructive reminder that external testing must precede clinical use. He et al. (https://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2026.1908970) studied nalbuphine-ropivacaine infiltration along the epidural puncture tract in a prospective cohort of 473 women undergoing cesarean delivery, reporting lower rates of post-neuraxial puncture-site pain alongside an interpretable XGBoost risk model (AUC 0.912); as the authors themselves note, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values describe statistical association within the model, not causal mechanism, so the treatment comparison still needs confirmation in an appropriately controlled trial.None of the above is clinically useful without infrastructure that turns knowledge into action at the point of prescribing, the kind of routinely collected, real-world clinical data that increasingly underpins broader evaluations of drug safety and effectiveness (Corrigan-Curay et al., 2018). Wu et al. (https://doi.org/10.3389/fped.2026.1736268) describe the construction of China's first Pediatric Rational Medication Intelligent Decision System, a database of more than 400,000 entries across 1,030 medications covering dosing, interactions, infusion parameters, pharmacogenomics, and compatibility with traditional Chinese medicines. Embedded into the hospital information system with real-time access to patient biochemical and disease data, the system was associated with a significant reduction in higher-severity prescription alerts. This contribution is a reminder that predictive algorithms and curated, governed knowledge bases are not competing approaches but necessary complements; sophisticated models still depend on structured, pediatric-specific, continuously maintained medication data to reach the bedside. It also highlights the adoption, implementation, and governance of the underlying systems that support this application, with the intention of improving patient outcomes.These six contributions sit within a much broader shift already reshaping drug discovery and clinical trial design (Paul et al., 2021) and raising ethical and regulatory questions across the clinical pharmacology discipline (Petkovic et al., 2024); obstetric and pediatric applications carry that broader shift into two of medicine's least evidence-rich populations. Across the six studies specifically, common priorities emerge: external validation across institutions, developmental stages, and healthcare systems; evaluation that extends beyond discrimination to calibration, transportability, workflow burden, and effect on patient outcomes; and transparent, explainable models with human clinical oversight retained wherever predictions influence dosing, monitoring, or intervention. These considerations carry strong emphasis when the populations affected are pregnant women, neonates, and children. The next challenge is translating these advances into routine pediatric and obstetric practice beyond specialist centers (Thompson et al., 2024).The contributions to this Research Topic show that AI and digital health can strengthen obstetric and pediatric pharmacology. The next phase of this work should shift from retrospective prediction and single-center feasibility to prospective evaluation and interoperable implementation. Ultimately, the success of artificial intelligence in obstetric and pediatric pharmacology will not be measured solely by algorithmic performance, but by its ability to support safer, more effective, and more individualized pharmacotherapy for mothers and children.
