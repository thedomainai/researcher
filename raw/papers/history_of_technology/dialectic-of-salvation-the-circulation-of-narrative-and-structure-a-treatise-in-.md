# Dialectic of Salvation: The Circulation of Narrative and Structure, A Treatise in Social Philosophy on the Relation Between World and Human Beings

**著者**: Vance Byun
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 技術史

## Abstract

This treatise develops a formal theory of the social life of narrative. It begins by examining how human beings sense and select movements of pre-narrative reality and experiential space, incorporate parts of those movements as information, and organize them into transmissible forms of knowledge structured by causality, value, and practical direction. It then traces how narratives circulate and compete, organize repeated practices, become institutionalized and internalized, harden into structures, and later collapse, lie dormant, mutate, or survive through institutional, practical, and symbolic residues. A narrative, in this account, is not merely a story or a subjective interpretation. It is a practical form of knowledge that binds multiple layers of experiential space into a causal structure and simultaneously organizes what is taken to be real, what counts as a good life, and what must be done to attain that life. The theoretical point of departure is a problem raised, but not fully generalized, by Max Weber in Die protestantische Ethik und der Geist des Kapitalismus. Weber showed how Calvinist narratives of salvation organized ascetic vocational conduct and entered into a selective affinity with the spirit of capitalism. He did not, however, fully formalize the conditions under which a narrative acquires binding force over conduct, nor did he provide a general theory of why practices and institutions produced by a narrative may continue to constrain actors after belief in the original narrative has weakened. This treatise extends Weber's problem beyond the particular case of Calvinism. It reconstructs it as a general process in which narratives organize practice, repeated practices become objectivated as structures, and those structures later return as constraints after their historical origins and initial purposes have been forgotten. This reversal—whereby a narrative constructed to save human beings or guide them toward a better life returns as a structure that constrains them—is termed the dialectic of salvation. Narratives acquire strong conduct-organizing force when the cycle of externalization, objectivation, and internalization hardens them into doxa: a self-evident reality that no longer appears to require questioning or justification. The layers of experiential space, however, do not change at the same rate. Social environments, including markets, technologies, institutions, labor regimes, housing systems, and media, may change relatively rapidly. Instrumental horizons of expectation adjust more slowly because they are sedimented in institutions, social networks, and repeated practices. State-oriented horizons of expectation—such as the pursuit of security, recognition, salvation, or a good life—may persist even longer because they remain connected to human telos and culturally embodied values. A narrative begins to collapse asymmetrically when transformations in the social environment undermine the factual adequacy of an established instrumental horizon while normative imperatives, state-oriented horizons, and institutional demands for performance remain in place. This treatise conceptualizes the resulting temporal disjuncture between structural transformation and narrative adjustment as V1. Narrative collapse is therefore not simply the disappearance of belief. It is a process in which a previously effective path ceases to produce its promised outcomes while persisting in institutions, relationships, norms, and actors' modes of self-evaluation. To analyze this process, the treatise proposes the V–Q–C framework, composed of three analytically irreducible axes. The V-axis describes the temporal, internalizational, and distributional configurations of narrative–structure relations. V1 refers to the temporal disjuncture between structural transformation and narrative adjustment; V2 to the intensity with which a narrative is internalized by an actor as doxic, self-evident reality; and V3 to the distributional breadth of a narrative across actor and institutional levels. The Q-axis evaluates the quality of existing and alternative narratives. Q1 refers to factual adequacy, Q2 to the internal coherence of core propositions, values, and practical directives, and Q3 to the structural and relational effects that a narrative produces or sustains. The C-axis describes the material and social conditions of action. C1 refers to capital that sustains survival and safety, C2 to capital that enables performance within a particular field, and C3 to capital that enables exit from an established path, transition to an alternative path, or transformation of the structure itself. Distinguishing these axes prevents the social distribution of a narrative, the quality of its claims, and actors' actual capacities for performance, survival, and exit from being treated as a single phenomenon. Different configurations of V, Q, and C generate five mechanisms of suffering. Suffering within a narrative arises when actors with high V2 repeatedly apply the narrative's standards of achievement as criteria of their own legitimacy. Double-bind suffering arises when the Q1 of an established instrumental horizon has declined, while normative imperatives and high V3 remain, and low C3 makes both compliance and exit costly. Anomic suffering emerges when an established doxa has lost its self-evidence but no alternative narrative with sufficient Q has formed to reconnect state-oriented horizons with practical paths. Suffering under structural coercion occurs when actors no longer believe the established narrative, yet its V3 distribution and coercive Q3 structures persist, while low C1 or C3 blocks the suspension of performance or departure from the established path. Guilt through legitimacy attack arises when multiple narratives remain active within a single actor and the meta-language of one narrative attacks the legitimacy of conduct organized by another. These types do not classify persons into fixed categories. They are ideal-typical mechanisms distinguishing how configurations of narrative, structure, capital, and meta-linguistic conflict generate suffering. The treatise consists of two parts. Part I constructs an integrated formal theory beginning with pre-narrative reality and experiential space and proceeding through horizons of expectation, social environments, the formation and internal structure of narrative, circulation and wars of ideas, narrative–structure circulation, the forgetting of origins, narrative collapse, the typology of suffering, the evaluation of narratives, and the theory's relation to existing traditions. Part II applies this framework to two distinct experiential spaces. The first case, the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism in early modern England, tests whether accumulated historical interpretations can be redescribed in the language of narrative–structure circulation without substantial loss of information. The second case, the life-course narrative and structural suffering in contemporary Korean society, tests the framework's generative capacity: whether it can organize transformations in education, labor, housing, family, and asset structures within a common formal coordinate system and distinguish mechanisms of suffering that existing accounts often conflate. The two cases are not identical in historical content or causal context. They are comparable at the level of form: narratives connect state-oriented and instrumental horizons, organize practices, become objectivated as structures, and later constrain actors after their origins have been forgotten. Methodologically, the treatise employs the Weberian ideal type as its principal analytical instrument. Rather than seeking closed-system causal laws, it formalizes generative mechanisms and demi-regular patterns that recur without recurring identically in open experiential spaces. Because doxa and the conditions represented by V, Q, and C cannot be observed through a single indicator, they must be examined indirectly through the convergence of institutional indicators, discursive materials, patterns of conduct, in-depth interviews, historical sources, and distributions of capital. The treatise is therefore not a completed empirical study but a work of theoretical groundwork—Grundlagenarbeit—for subsequent historical-sociological, qualitative, and quantitative research. Its contribution lies not in proposing a closed grand theory of social reality, but in providing a formal language through which narrative collapse, the inertia of doxa, structural coercion, anomie, self-blame, guilt, and unequal capacities for exit may be comparatively analyzed. The treatise also acknowledges that its own theory is itself a form of knowledge produced through the selective organization of movements within experiential space. Narrative–structure circulation, the forgetting of origins, the V–Q–C framework, and the typology of suffering are not reality itself, but formal languages that provisionally stabilize parts of reality in order to render them intelligible. They must therefore remain open to revision through new evidence, counterexamples, and encounters with other experiential spaces. The practical implication of the theory is not the imposition of a new correct narrative. What is required after narrative collapse is the cultivation of Bildung and a narrative ecology in which multiple narratives can be compared, evaluated through Q1, Q2, and Q3, safely abandoned when they fail, and experimentally reconstructed. The question is therefore not whether human beings should possess narratives at all, but which narratives should be chosen, under what conditions and critical procedures, and with what responsibility for the structures they produce.
