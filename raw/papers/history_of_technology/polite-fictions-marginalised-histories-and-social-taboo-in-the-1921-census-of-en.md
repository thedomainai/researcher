# Polite Fictions: Marginalised Histories and Social Taboo in the 1921 Census of England and Wales

**著者**: Jane Skelding
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 技術史

## Abstract

The thesis uses a combination of digital methods and social history to identify patterns of colloquial language in the 1921 census responses to help recover histories which were marginalised by contemporary society and subsequently by historical research. Deciphering irregular and ‘messy’ data (i.e., data with spelling errors, inconsistent formats, or missing values), this analysis of the census language reveals that both state representatives (enumerators, records office staff, and registrars) and householders employed phrases that were ambiguous and served as face-saving euphemisms, used within the codified rules of the census. The resulting case studies cover the following phrases: ‘grass widow’, ‘joint head’, ‘housekeeper’, ‘unmarried wife’, and ‘shell-shock’: census responses which constitute ‘polite fictions’ that reflected taboo behaviours and have contributed to the marginalisation of certain groups within the archives, and, subsequently, historical scholarship. The thesis decodes these phrases in their contemporary usage, employing methods from digital humanities, social history, literary analysis, and linguistics. This detailed analysis of the census is significant for historians because it provides evidence that the non-normative language is not just a quirk or an aberration but reflects the power relationships within the bureaucratic language game of the census, reflecting society more broadly and making the census a more viable source for marginalised histories. However, the factors which contribute to archival silences and erasures began before the 1921 census and continue into the present day. For this reason, the history of these phrases before 1921, their specific social and cultural context in the post-war period, and present-day digitisation methods are mapped as a continuous process of mediated curation. Analysing all of these issues within both the corpus of the census and the wider cultural corpus over the long term, the case studies demonstrate how we can use this approach to understand and write nuanced histories of relationships, sexuality, and disability that would otherwise be obscured by what one contemporary described as the ‘statistical morass’ of the 1921 census.
