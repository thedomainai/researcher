## Abstract

Digital technologies have become part of everyday working lives.Employees across sectors use Slack or WhatsApp to communicate, email dominates the workday, factory workers in Industry 4.0 produce at rhythms dictated by algorithms, and some workers do not even have a human boss anymore.These workers are independent contractors in the digital gig economy (Uber, Deliveroo, Taskrabbit, etc.), where they receive orders and payments from a smartphone app.Labor is relevant to machinic vision in two ways.On the one hand, the entanglement of workers and machines in the labor process has ushered in debates about who can be considered "subjects of labor."Who ultimately occupies the seat of control in the labor process: human workers or machines?Just as visual culture has too often identified human beings as full-fledged subjects while reducing machines to the role of instrumental objects (Johnston 1999, 27), so labor theorists regularly assume that workers are autonomous subjects whereas their tools are mere instruments.Today's digitally mediated workplaces undercut this hierarchy in favor of complexes of human and nonhuman agents working together as assemblages (Lazzarato 2014).On the other hand, labor is also an object of machinic vision.Digital technologies have created new differential regimes of visibility in the workplace in which some forms of work become hypervisible to supervisors, whereas others are rendered invisible in order to hide exploitative working conditions from consumers.Marx's Grundrisse provides insight into the matter of labor subjectivity, specifically his "Fragment on Machines," which the Italian workerist journal Quaderni rossi published separately in 1964 (Marx 2005;Tronti 2008).Marx discusses the impact of industrial automation and hypothesizes the imminent dissolution of the dichotomy between human subject and machinic object for the future of work.In traditional, craft-centered manufacturing, human artisans determined what was
