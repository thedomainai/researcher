# Coupled Evolution How Interacting Systems Become Causes of Each Other's Change

**著者**: 政恩 馮
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 進化生物学・文化進化

## Abstract

Description Coupled Evolution develops a general framework for understanding how interacting systems can become causes of one another’s change. The central idea is simple: evolution does not always occur only within isolated systems. Sometimes change emerges because systems become coupled. When two or more systems interact repeatedly, each system can alter the conditions under which the others continue to develop. Their relationship can influence direction, speed, stability, available pathways, and future possibilities. A general coupled system can be represented as: A(t+1) = F(A(t), B(t), R(t), U(t)) B(t+1) = G(B(t), A(t), R(t), V(t)) R(t+1) = H(R(t), A(t), B(t), W(t)) where A and B represent interacting systems, and R represents the evolving relationship or coupling structure between them. This leads to a key proposition: the systems may change,the relationship may change,and the changing relationship may alter how future change becomes possible. Coupled Evolution therefore distinguishes three layers: System ChangeRelation ChangeCoupling-Architecture Change The framework emphasizes that interaction alone is not sufficient to establish coupled evolution. A meaningful coupling should demonstrate measurable reciprocal or directional influence, identifiable causal pathways, temporal structure, and explanatory value beyond simpler independent-system models. For this reason, one of the central methodological principles of the framework is: Coupling should be estimated, not presumed. The work develops a broad taxonomy of coupling, including weak and strong coupling, symmetric and asymmetric coupling, positive and negative feedback, delayed coupling, hierarchical coupling, network coupling, adaptive coupling, and dynamic coupling in which the strength or structure of the relationship itself changes over time. This makes it possible to ask not only: How does system A change? or: How does system B change? but also: How does the relationship between A and B evolve? And: How does that evolving relationship reshape the future state space of both systems? The framework connects this idea with existing scientific traditions including coevolution, evolutionary ecology, niche construction, systems biology, complex adaptive systems, network theory, dynamical systems, cultural evolution, ecological inheritance, host–microbiome interactions, social systems, technological change, and human–AI interaction. These fields are not treated as proof of a single universal mechanism. Instead, they provide empirical domains in which coupled evolutionary dynamics can be tested, compared, and potentially falsified. Examples include: organisms and ecosystems,hosts and microbiomes,humans and technologies,cultures and institutions,cities and populations,markets and information systems,social groups and communication networks,humans and artificial intelligence. In each case, the central question is whether the interaction produces persistent reciprocal influence that modifies future trajectories. A second major proposition follows: relationships can acquire history. Repeated interaction may generate path dependence, memory, specialization, constraint, dependence, coordination, or mutual adaptation. The coupling itself can therefore become part of the system’s inherited structure. This suggests that evolutionary history may exist not only inside individual entities, but also inside the relationships among them. The book develops formal tools for analyzing these processes, including coupling matrices, dynamical equations, causal graphs, state-space models, timescale separation, network representations, perturbation tests, null models, coupling-strength estimation, and model comparison. A valid coupled-evolution model should outperform a simpler independent-system explanation. If removing the coupling term produces no meaningful loss of explanatory or predictive power, then the coupling hypothesis may be unnecessary. For this reason, the framework includes explicit failure conditions and kill criteria. Coupled Evolution is not presented as a replacement for established evolutionary biology. Nor does it claim that biological, cultural, technological, ecological, and institutional change all operate through identical mechanisms. Instead, it proposes a broader structural question: when multiple systems repeatedly influence one another, can the interaction itself become an evolving causal structure? The framework can therefore be summarized as: Evolution Through Coupling System ↔ SystemRelation ↔ RelationArchitecture ↔ Future Possibility The deeper claim is: change does not always arise only from what a system is. Sometimes change arises from what the system is connected to, how strongly it is connected, and how that connection changes over time. In this sense: Evolution does not only happen to systems. Sometimes systems become causes of each other’s change.
