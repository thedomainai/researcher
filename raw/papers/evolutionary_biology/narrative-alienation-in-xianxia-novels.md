# Narrative Alienation in Xianxia Novels

**著者**: Yan Li
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 進化生物学・文化進化

## Abstract

This paper theorizes narrative alienation in early twenty‑first‑century male‑oriented xianxia novels as a dialectical tension between Confucian‑Taoist ethical frameworks and the imperatives of digital commercialization. The analysis draws on Husserl’s phenomenology of internal time‑consciousness, Bourdieu’s theory of the literary field, and the Marxian concept of reification. The central argument is that the genre’s invocation of Taoist immortality generates a form of “temporal alienation”: infinite lifespans dissolve the existential urgency inherent in Confucian self‑cultivation, consigning protagonists to a phenomenological state of arrested development that parallels Erikson’s notion of “identity moratorium.” Confucian ethics, in turn, are reified into utilitarian signifiers—social hierarchies and familial obligations are reduced to pretexts for vengeance and intra‑sectarian power struggles—while the Taoist practice of xingming shuangxiu (cultivation of both nature and life‑force) is flattened into a mechanistic scheme of realm progression, thereby contravening the spiritual precepts of Neidan (inner alchemy). Commercialization intensifies this process through algorithm‑driven prioritization of “pleasure points,” which compresses ethical complexity into readily marketable tropes—a phenomenon that exemplifies Baudrillardian symbolic consumption. The gendered dimensions of narrative strategy further reveal a paradoxical divergence: female‑oriented subgenres reconstruct the relationship between qing (emotion) and the Dao as a means of resisting generic homogenization, whereas male‑oriented works remain entrapped in a cyclical logic of utilitarian violence. The study concludes that overcoming narrative alienation requires a renegotiation of the literary field’s autonomy—one that reimagines Confucian‑Taoist ethics not as commodified emblems but as generative sources of narrative conflict—and that a phenomenological recovery of time’s constitutive role in ethical identity is essential for the genre’s renewal.
