# Superintelligence Series Volume 5: Intelligent Visual Arts and Audiovisual Engineering: Generative AI, Computational Creativity and Superintelligence

**著者**: Emanuel Maldonado
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 進化生物学・文化進化

## Abstract

An image can now begin with a sentence.A moving scene can be assembled from generated frames, a three-dimensional environment reconstructed from photographs, and a recorded voice adapted to another language.These possibilities have altered the working conditions of visual creation.They have also made familiar questions more difficult.Who decides whether an image is convincing?What does it mean for a model to be open?How should a filmmaker weigh technical assistance against control over the finished work?And what obligations arise when synthetic images enter public collections or circulate without their origins being clear?Such questions belong as much to the humanities and social sciences as they do to computer science.The aesthetic value of an image cannot be read directly from a benchmark score.A license expresses rights and restrictions that may be obscured by the broad language of openness.A generated portrait may display technical precision while reproducing a narrow view of who belongs in a particular profession.Even the apparently straightforward task of identifying synthetic content becomes uncertain when a detector encounters a model different from those on which it was trained.Technical capability and human judgment meet at each of these points, though they do not always agree.The chapters brought together here examine this relationship through different forms of evidence.Some study model registries, image databases and public repositories; others compare measurements of visual quality with human assessments or test the transfer of forensic methods across generators.The reviews turn to the evaluation of generated and restored video, the reconstruction of scenes through neural radiance fields and 3D Gaussian splatting, and the presence of machine learning in editing, color grading, sound, dubbing and lip synchronization.Accounts of social bias, authorship and copyright bring the discussion back to the people and institutions affected by synthetic media.The value of reading these subjects together lies in the questions they share.A technically plausible result may be perceptually weak.A method that performs well on one dataset may behave differently elsewhere.Tools that reduce repetitive work can leave the central artistic decision unresolved.The conditions under which a model is distributed can be as consequential as the images it produces.These are not merely temporary inconveniences on the way to more capable systems.They concern the ways visual work is assessed, attributed, maintained and trusted.Readers will encounter different levels of certainty across the chapters.Empirical findings depend on their samples, dates and measures; reviews reflect the scope of the literature they examine.Legal treatment varies by jurisdiction, and professional practices differ between production settings.The book treats those limits as part of the subject, because claims about creative automation become more useful when the conditions under which they hold are visible.The pages that follow are intended for researchers, artists, audiovisual practitioners and readers interested in the institutions surrounding digital culture.They invite a careful view of computational creativity: one attentive to what machines can produce, to what their outputs leave unanswered, and to the human choices that still give visual work its purpose.Once an image is generated, a different problem arises: how should its quality be measured?Traditional image-quality metrics were developed largely around properties of natural images and their distortions.Generated images may fail in ways these measures do not capture, including weak correspondence to a prompt or a visually plausible composition whose details make little sense.The comparison of BRISQUE, NIQE and PIQE with human judgments illustrates both the appeal and the limits of inexpensive measures that require no reference image.The systematic review of video-generation evaluation extends the concern across time, where individual frames may appear convincing while movement, continuity or narrative coherence remains unsatisfactory.Metrics are necessary for comparison, but their meaning depends on the quality of the human judgment they are expected to approximate.Questions of trust run alongside questions of quality.Synthetic images can be difficult to distinguish from photographs, and forensic methods seek patterns that a human observer may not see.Frequency-domain detectors offer one way to identify traces of generation.Their performance across unfamiliar generators, however, shows why a strong result under controlled conditions cannot be assumed to hold across the wider field.The study of machine-generated images on Wikimedia Commons places a related issue in a public setting.Uploading an image to a shared repository does not ensure its meaningful reuse, and counting files is not the same as measuring their value to a community.Provenance, moderation and actual use must therefore be considered separately.The social content of generated images demands another form of assessment.Models trained on large collections of images can carry familiar stereotypes into new visual material.The review of audit studies on text-to-image generation examines how representations of gender, race and culture have been measured, and why documenting a bias is easier than demonstrating that an intervention has reduced it.This is an artistic concern as well as an ethical one.When the available image of a scientist, a caregiver or a professional authority repeatedly follows a narrow pattern, generative systems can constrain the imaginative possibilities that they appear to expand.Authorship presents a further tension.A generated work may involve the selection of data, the writing and revision of prompts, the curation of outputs, and extensive human editing.The philosophical account of creativity may recognize these contributions as distributed across people and tools.Copyright systems, by contrast, must apply legal criteria to particular claims of authorship and protection.The chapter on machine-generated visual art traces how these questions have developed and why the threshold of human contribution and the treatment of training material remain unsettled.These issues cannot be answered solely by examining what a model produces.They require a view of the process by which the work came into being.Audiovisual engineering brings the debate into the production environment.Neural radiance fields and 3D Gaussian splatting make it possible to represent and render scenes through markedly different computational approaches.Their respective trade-offs in fidelity, speed, editability and temporal stability show that the preferred method depends on the work to be done.A related lesson emerges from the review of video super-resolution and restoration: favorable benchmark scores reveal only part of a method's suitability when computational cost, data diversity and actual viewing conditions are inconsistently reported.In post-production, machine learning is already present across editing, color grading, sound, dubbing and lip synchronization, but the need for continuity, directability and artistic judgment remains.A workflow can become less laborious without becoming less dependent on human intention.Taken together, the chapters connect four questions: what systems make possible, how their performance can be established, whose interests their outputs affect, and who remains accountable for the result.They also reveal a recurring mismatch between what is easy to count and what matters in practice.A model may be downloadable yet legally restricted; a score may improve without matching perception; an archive may grow without widespread reuse; a generated sequence may look persuasive frame by frame but fail in motion.These distinctions are essential to the interpretation of evidence in a field where demonstrations are often more visible than their limitations.The prospect of increasingly autonomous artificial intelligence makes such distinctions more pressing, not less.The material examined here concerns existing methods and the evidence available to assess them.It does not treat speculative capabilities as established outcomes.Its purpose is to situate generative visual systems within the wider work of creation, evaluation and public accountability.The reader is invited to move between computation and culture without assuming that either perspective can explain the whole of contemporary visual production.
