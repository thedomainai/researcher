# The Open Neural Observatory Non-Invasive Brain-Computer Interfaces as a General Scientific Instrument for Longitudinal Human Neuroscience

**著者**: 政恩 馮
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 脳科学

## Abstract

Description The Open Neural Observatory proposes a civilization-scale research architecture for transforming non-invasive brain-computer interfaces from task-specific control systems into general scientific instruments for observing human neural dynamics across time. The central question of this work is not simply: “How can the brain control a computer?” It is: “Can non-invasive neural technologies become a long-term scientific observatory for studying how human brains learn, adapt, age, recover, and change?” The framework therefore reframes brain-computer interfaces through a three-stage transition: BCI→ Scientific Instrument→ Longitudinal Neural Observatory Traditional neuroscience often studies people during relatively short laboratory sessions. Such experiments are scientifically valuable, but they provide only limited views of neural change across months, years, different environments, developmental stages, illnesses, learning processes, or recovery trajectories. A mature non-invasive neural observatory could complement these methods by enabling repeated, standardized, longitudinal measurements across large populations while preserving autonomy, privacy, and psychological integrity. The research architecture is organized around six foundational structures: 1. Neural Measurement Infrastructure This domain examines the measurement layer required for reliable longitudinal neuroscience, including high-density EEG, dry and wearable sensors, EEG–fNIRS integration, multimodal physiological sensing, cross-device calibration, artifact correction, and long-term individual baselines. The scientific objective is to move from isolated neural recordings toward comparable neural time series. Instead of: one person→ one experiment→ one recording the long-term goal becomes: one person→ repeated measurements→ months and years→ measurable neural trajectories. 2. Neural Foundation Models Large-scale longitudinal neural data creates the possibility of general models that learn reusable representations of brain activity across participants, tasks, devices, and contexts. The framework explores: general EEG representations, cross-subject transfer, cross-task learning, personal neural models, brain-state embeddings, uncertainty calibration, and interpretable neural representation spaces. Rather than building a separate model for every experiment, future systems may learn general neural representations that can later be adapted to specific scientific questions. This creates a transition from task-specific decoding toward reusable neural modeling infrastructure. 3. Closed-Loop Causal Neuroscience Observation alone cannot establish causation. The Open Neural Observatory therefore places strong emphasis on closed-loop experimental systems. A general scientific cycle is proposed: Measure→ Model→ Predict→ Intervene→ Measure Again→ Verify Such systems may combine real-time neural-state estimation with adaptive experiments, neurofeedback, behavioral interventions, or carefully governed non-invasive stimulation. The objective is not merely to identify correlations between brain activity and behavior. It is to develop controlled experimental architectures capable of testing whether changes in neural states reliably produce changes in cognition, behavior, recovery, or learning. A core scientific distinction therefore runs throughout the framework: Prediction ≠ Causation 4. Longitudinal Human Neuroscience The most important scientific opportunity may be the systematic study of neural change across long periods of human life. Potential research directions include: individual neural baselines, learning-induced neural plasticity, brain aging trajectories, sleep–stress–cognition coupling, developmental transitions, recovery after neurological injury, and early detection of meaningful deviations from individual baselines. This creates a new scientific question: What becomes visible when neuroscience studies not only populations, but trajectories? Population averages remain essential, but longitudinal measurements may allow researchers to distinguish individual variation from genuine within-person change. The framework therefore treats the human brain not as a static object, but as a continuously evolving dynamical system. 5. Precision Neurorehabilitation Non-invasive BCI technologies may become particularly valuable when measurement and intervention are connected to rehabilitation. The framework explores applications including: stroke recovery, motor rehabilitation, communication assistance, neuroplasticity monitoring, adaptive BCI training, and closed-loop rehabilitation systems. The long-term objective is not simply to maximize decoding accuracy. It is to understand how different individuals recover, which interventions work for whom, and how neural adaptation changes during rehabilitation. This requires personalized models, longitudinal monitoring, and explicit measurement of uncertainty. 6. Open Neural Science and Neuro-Rights A planetary neural observatory would create scientific opportunities but also unprecedented ethical risks. Neural data may contain highly sensitive information about health, cognition, behavior, and internal states. The framework therefore treats governance as part of the scientific architecture rather than as an external ethical appendix. Core principles include: informed consent, mental privacy, neural data sovereignty, data minimization, transparent models, right to refusal, right to exit, right to deletion where technically and legally possible, fair access, independent oversight, and protection of psychological integrity. A central boundary of the framework is: Non-Invasive BCI ≠ Arbitrary Mind Reading Current neural technologies do not provide unrestricted access to private thoughts, and scientific communication should avoid exaggerating what neural decoding can establish. Likewise: More Data ≠ More Science Large neural datasets are valuable only when accompanied by reliable measurement, explicit hypotheses, strong baselines, reproducible methods, uncertainty estimates, and independent validation. The Open Neural Observatory therefore proposes a broader scientific philosophy: Scientific Progress ≤ Human Cognitive Sovereignty Scientific capability should expand without turning the human nervous system into an involuntary source of extractive data. The framework envisions an open research infrastructure in which participants are not merely data sources, but rights-bearing contributors to a shared scientific project. The long-term analogy is closer to an astronomical observatory than to a consumer interface. A telescope does not control the universe. It creates a stable instrument through which previously invisible phenomena become observable. In the same way, the greatest scientific contribution of non-invasive brain-computer interfaces may eventually be not faster device control, but the creation of a new observational layer for human neuroscience. The resulting Human Neural Observatory could connect: neuroscience, cognitive science, psychology, sleep research, education, aging research, neurology, psychiatry, rehabilitation medicine, artificial intelligence, and human-computer interaction. The framework is organized across thirty research axes and further develops candidate propositions, measurement architectures, failure modes, comparative study designs, research programs, and a 2026–2056 development roadmap. Its central proposition can be summarized as follows: The future of non-invasive brain-computer interfaces may not be defined by how effectively humans control machines. It may be defined by how carefully humanity learns to observe its own changing brain. The Open Neural Observatory therefore proposes a shift: from interface engineeringto scientific instrumentation, from short experimentsto longitudinal trajectories, from isolated datasetsto interoperable neural infrastructure, from predictionto causal verification, and from unrestricted data extractionto neuroscience governed by cognitive sovereignty. Its deepest objective is not to make the brain more transparent at any cost. It is to make human neural change more scientifically observable while keeping the human person free.
