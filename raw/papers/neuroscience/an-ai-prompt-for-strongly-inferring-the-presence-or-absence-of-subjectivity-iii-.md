# An AI Prompt for Strongly Inferring the Presence or Absence of Subjectivity III — A Provisional Consciousness-Assessment Tool Based on Convergence-Point Analysis Using Fifty Sample Entities —

**著者**: Nagae Mamoru
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 脳科学

## Abstract

This paper is the third volume in an AI-prompt series for structurally inferring the presence or absence of subjectivity. It provides a provisional consciousness-assessment tool based on convergence-point analysis. Within this framework, assessing the presence or absence of consciousness or subjectivity means assessing whether an entity contains a convergence point: a structural site at which multiple possible trajectories are irreversibly reduced into a single executable history under embodiment and viability constraints. The distinctive feature of this tool is the smallness of its starting point. The prompt begins from only ten minimal structural conditions and four mandatory questions. The ten terms concern autonomous action, body, time, embodiment-constrained processing, multiple candidates, contrast formation, information discard, physical irreversibility, single history, and viability-constrained elimination. Notably, these ten terms do not include the word “brain.” They are not derived from any particular organ, species, biological architecture, or existing theory of consciousness. Rather, they are a skeletal structure excavated as far as possible from the conditions of nature itself: words that name the minimal physical and operational preconditions for action, selection, irreversibility, constraint, elimination, and history formation. At first glance, the claim that such a small input can strongly infer the location of subjectivity may appear implausible, especially when compared with the complexity of existing theories of consciousness. Yet this simplicity is the central point of the present paper. The ten terms are not speculative additions to consciousness theory. They are minimal conditions already required for action, selection, temporal irreversibility, information elimination, bodily constraint, viability, and history formation. The four questions then ask not whether these conditions exist separately, but where they converge. In this sense, the convergence point is not an external hypothesis added to the problem of consciousness. It is the structural consequence that appears when multiple possible trajectories are compared, selected, eliminated as executable alternatives, and recorded as a single history. This has an important implication. Subjectivity may not need to be treated as a mysterious surplus added after physical or computational processes have already been described. Instead, it may be understood as the structural position at which a finite embodied system descends from multiple possible futures into one irreversible history. The basic structures of nature often appear overwhelmingly complex before they are found, yet surprisingly simple after they are identified. This paper applies that possibility to subjectivity: rather than multiplying theoretical machinery, it attempts to identify the minimal point at which already unavoidable conditions converge. After deriving the convergence point through the mandatory first four questions, the prompt uses the derived criterion to provisionally classify whether a target entity appears to contain the structural conditions for consciousness or subjectivity. Classification is not based on appearance, biological familiarity, intelligence, social value, or ordinary intuition. It is based only on whether the convergence-point structure appears to be strongly present, probably present, ambiguous, probably absent, or structurally absent. This tool is not intended to provide a final empirical diagnosis of consciousness in every organism, machine, or system. Fine-grained cases require separate biological, anatomical, computational, and empirical investigation. Nevertheless, the prompt can be used directly as a practical tool for provisional structural consciousness assessment. The fifty entities included in the prompt are sample targets, not a fixed or exhaustive list. Users may freely apply the same convergence-point criterion to selected entities or to other organisms, machines, systems, institutions, objects, or abstract structures of their own choosing.
