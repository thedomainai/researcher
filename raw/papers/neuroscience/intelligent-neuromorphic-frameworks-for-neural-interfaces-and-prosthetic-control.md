# Intelligent Neuromorphic Frameworks for Neural Interfaces and Prosthetic Control

**著者**: Aditya Dayal Tyagi, Himani Tyagi, Setu Garg, Vivek Tomar
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 脳科学

## Abstract

The convergence of neuroscience and artificial intelligence is showing phenomenal horizons in creating brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) and neuroprosthetic devices. The highlight of this transformation is neuromorphic computing- a new computing paradigm which is inspired by the structure and functionality of the human brain. This chapter will discuss the roles that neuromorphic systems and specifically spiking neural networks (SNNs) are changing the neural signal processing neural landscape by providing an energymorphic mix of efficiency, flexibility, and real-time capabilities. We start by putting the discussion into the biological context of the principles that have guided the design of neuromorphic hardware and algorithms. Neuromorphic systems event-drive and distributed, unlike conventional computing systems based on centralized, clock-driven designs. This change allows more natural compatibility with neural signals like EEG, EMG, and ECoG and thus they are especially well suited to use in BCIs and assistive prosthetics. The chapter provides an overview of the design of neuromorphic chips to operate on sparse, noisy neural data in real-time and with very low power constraints through a thorough analysis of current hardware systems such as the Intel Loihi, the IBM TrueNorth, the SpiNNaker, and the BrainScaleS systems. Such systems are able to interpret complicated motor intentions, cognitive states, or sensory signals and convert them into viable commands to operate prosthetic limbs, cochlear implants, or retinal interfaces. In addition, learning algorithms like the spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) enable these systems to change with time and become more personal and natural in human-machine interaction. In addition to the technical basis, we also discuss how neuromorphic BCIs are creating closed-loop feedback systems that can dynamically react to the neural activity of the user. These capabilities not only promise to make prosthetics control more precise and responsive, but also are opening the door to bi- directional interfaces that could one day restore the lost sense capabilities. The chapter concludes by addressing the critical challenges that remain, including variability in brain signals, integration into wearable or implantable formats, and the need for ethical oversight in clinical translation. We also highlight future directions such as federated neuromorphic learning, embedded edge AI for BCIs, and the exciting convergence with brain organoid models. In essence, neuromorphic systems represent a transformative step toward truly brain-inspired technologies that can bridge the gap between biology and machine—ultimately enabling more natural, adaptive, and accessible neurotechnological solutions.
