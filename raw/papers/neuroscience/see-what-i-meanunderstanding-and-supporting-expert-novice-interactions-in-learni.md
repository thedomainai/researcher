# See what I mean?:Understanding and supporting expert-novice interactions in learning complex visual tasks

**著者**: Christine; id_orcid 0000-0002-4344-1450 van Nooijen
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 脳科学

## Abstract

Learning how to complete complex visual tasks in adult or professional learning contexts is difficult; not just due to the inherently complex nature of the visual tasks themselves, but also due to the manner in which these types of tasks are typically learned. In many cases, the best or only way for a novice to learn is through one-on-one interaction with an expert. Experts hold a wealth of knowledge and expertise, however this knowledge is interconnected and can be implicit, which makes it difficult to convey to a novice especially with little to no didactic training. The aims of this dissertation were twofold; to understand and support expert-novice interactions in visual learning contexts. In Chapter 2, we conducted a scoping review that established a framework for scaffolding informed by cognitive psychology including models of working memory and cognitive load theory. These findings showed that experts scaffold novices through cueing behaviours (directing attention) and chunking behaviours (connecting information). In Chapter 3, subjective experiences of joint visual attention (JVA) generally corresponded with objective eye-tracking measures, supporting the use of dual eye-tracking as a useful but not entirely complete proxy for JVA. In Chapter 4, during forensic expert-novice interaction, expert cueing was the most commonly occurring scaffolding behaviour, and expert cues were more prevalent in segments of high JVA relative to segments of low JVA. In Chapter 5, an EMME intervention for first year policing students showed no significant effects on hazard detection reaction time or accuracy. In Chapter 6, a VR-museum based instructional intervention with conversational AI successfully influenced the types of questions asked by participants in different conditions; however, these changes do not necessarily translate into broader differences in question behaviour, attention allocation or learning outcomes. Our findings suggest successful scaffolding of novice learning in complex visual tasks is not solely derived from the expert’s capacity to scaffold, but a product of the interaction itself, whereby novices also have a task of making their understanding (or lack thereof) visible. Neither participants in the dyad can ‘accomplish’ a successful interaction alone. JVA data operationalised by dual eye-tracking can provide rich data, which in turn may provide insights into dyadic expert-novice interactions in visual learning contexts. Together, these chapters build a multi-context account of how visual expertise development is scaffolded in one-to-one interaction, how it can be captured through scaffolding behaviours like speech and gestures, eye-tracking metrics like joint visual attention, and how emerging technologies may support it under specific conditions.
