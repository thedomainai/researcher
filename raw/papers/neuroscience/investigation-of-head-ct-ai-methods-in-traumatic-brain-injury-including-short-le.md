# Investigation of Head CT AI Methods In Traumatic Brain Injury Including Short Learning Effects

**著者**: Joel Toledo-Urena
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 脳科学

## Abstract

Computed tomography (CT) of the brain is an essential tool for evaluating various pathologies. In emergent care, a head CT (HCT) scan is often the preferred imaging modality for assessing a patient due to the rapid acquisition speed, which allows for quick assessment and timely decision making in critical situations. This imaging modality is specifically beneficial in traumatic brain injury (TBI) cases, where imaging data are vital to determining the patient’s treatment plan and care, as well as for potentially investigating neurological symptoms in patients experiencing long COVID-19. However, interpreting and evaluating HCT scans can take time and require trained experts to ensure accurate assessment. Thus, by developing automated computer techniques, we aim to improve HCT scan assessment to ultimately enhance patient treatment quality and outcomes. Since the 1980’s, computer-aided diagnosis techniques have been developed to aid healthcare professionals in evaluating patient data in a more effective scope. Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have significantly improved diagnostic accuracy and streamlined clinical workflows, demonstrating effectiveness and efficiency across various healthcare applications. One such advancement is that of foundation models, which encode input data for a variety of downstream tasks. Therefore, we propose the use of machine learning methods to quantitatively evaluate HCT scans acquired from patients with TBI and COVID-19. HCT scans often include extraneous information that can have an impact on the performance of machine learning models, such as causing an algorithm to reach an erroneous prediction. Such non-patient content may include medical devices irrelevant to the task, the CT bed, and other foreign bodies. Manual segmentation of brain anatomy is rarely performed in clinical settings due to its impracticality. However, if computational techniques could automate this process, it could be a useful tool in an overall AI system for enhancing patient evaluation. Therefore, we developed a U-Net based cross-validation ensemble method to segment the brain in TBI patient CT scans, including accurate management of areas with high tissue damage and/or image artifacts, allowing for consistent downstream evaluation focusing only on the brain matter. Deep learning architectures encode input data into embeddings, which can be used for clinically relevant downstream tasks. However, deep learning architectures are susceptible to shortcut learning, in which the model encodes spurious information, degrading downstream performance. Assessing learned embedding representations using cluster analysis techniques, such as dimensionality reduction and cluster metrics, allows evaluation of underlying patterns and shortcuts in the data. Despite the availability of various dimensionality reduction techniques and clustering metrics, there is a lack of consensus and consistency among methods for evaluating extracted embeddings and underlying patterns. Thus, we developed the Cluster Analysis Tool (CAT) to streamline the analysis of extracted embeddings from deep learning architectures to dimensions not accessible to the human eye. CAT was capable of performing dimensionality reduction and calculating cluster analysis metrics to provide both qualitative and quantitative assessment. To evaluate CAT’s ability to handle inputs of varying complexity and structure, we tested the tool on both the Iris dataset, a set of low-dimensional features, and a TBI clinical dataset, consisting of high-dimensional embeddings extracted from deep learning architectures. HCT scans are a vital diagnostic tool that captures 3D information that allows for lifesaving decisions to be made. There are many different scanner models, vendors, acquisition parameters, and patient populations, and AI architectures may encode spurious correlations rather than clinically relevant information. Thus, the final aim of this dissertation was to assess extracted embeddings from three different pretrained models: ResNet50, pretrained on ImageNet; RAD-DINO, pretrained on chest radiographs; and CT-FM, pretrained on CT scans. Embeddings were extracted from HCT scans in the COVID-19 Data Mart and TXA TBI Trial datasets. We used the clustering methods employed by CAT to qualitatively and quantitatively assess the extracted embeddings. Furthermore, we evaluated the impact of finetuning on embedded representations by utilizing the SinoCT dataset. All pretrained models exhibit vendor-level embedding clustering, both qualitatively and quantitatively. Finetuning reduced vendor level embedding clustering for the COVID-19 Data Mart scans, while the TXA TBI Trial dataset embeddings still clustered by vendor, i.e., still demonstrated a shortcut behavior. The work in this dissertation demonstrates how AI methods can be applied for data preparation and embedding evaluation in HCT scans of TBI and COVID-19 patients. This included applying deep learning to segment complex presentations of TBI HCT scans, developing CAT to streamline qualitative and quantitative embedding cluster analysis, and using CAT methods to evaluate vendor-based shortcuts in HCT embeddings, demonstrating how finetuning reshaped embeddings and influenced downstream classification. Methods developed in this work highlight the importance of upstream analysis in supporting the development of reliable and safe applications of AI in medicine.
