# The Moral Code of Reality: Physical Structure, Agency, and the Conditions of Living Together

**著者**: Jonathan Washburn
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 哲学

## Abstract

From the book's opening: Someone needs help. Someone else can give it. Between those two facts lies much of moral life: what help changes, what it costs, who can provide it, what has been promised, and whether both people can go on afterward. Love belongs at the center of this account. It directs attention to a damaged relation and the possibility of repair. In Recognition Science that possibility has an exact physical form. A relation compares quantities, its imbalance has a cost, and an action changes it. For a pair of positive comparison ratios whose product is conserved, equalizing the ratios gives the unique endpoint of least total recognition cost. Love names this conserving repair. The result concerns the same bonds and transformations that physics describes. The theorem makes one part of love precise. Human love also involves attention, affection, fidelity and the ability to keep caring through time. Explaining those capacities requires the people, their histories and their means of action to remain in view. A lower total burden can leave the person helping unable to continue. A perfectly balanced record can conceal an unkept promise. A welcome effect can occur without the recipient's consent. Each fact changes what a loving action must attend to. The virtues describe different parts of this work. This book develops fourteen named virtues, with Love among them. Their number is the size of the catalogue being examined; the physical results establish the operations and their conditions, without deriving fourteen as the number of every possible virtue. These descriptions say what each virtue has to accomplish. The chapters distinguish what an operation already establishes from what its fuller realization still needs. Love has a conserving uniqueness theorem. Justice has an exact execution on shared bonds once the correction is specified. Care has exact conditions for finite continuation in its stated environment. Several other rules retain chosen response rates or depend on an uncompleted identification. A standing disposition also requires a person whose records and capacities reliably select the fitting operation. A named operation alone does not establish that disposition. A physical moral structure can leave more than one action available, and sometimes more than one action meets the same requirement. Its necessity lies in the relations every adequate judgment must preserve: people and their needs, actual effects, scarce resources, authored commitments and the history that connects them. Understanding those relations gives us something definite to repair, sustain and answer for.
