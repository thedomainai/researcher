# The Life of Text in the Age of AI: A UPCT-Based Theory of Essential Extraction through d(ΦR)/dt ≥ 0

**著者**: Kazunori Ohumi
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 哲学

## Abstract

One-Sentence Central Claim In the age of AI-generated textual flooding, the essence of knowledge is not the most accurate, polished, cited, or institutionally validated text, but the living text that continues to increase generative possibility Φ and relational resonance R over time, expressed by d(ΦR)/dt ≥ 0. Description Generative AI has radically lowered the cost of producing academic papers, summaries, reports, policy documents, literary works, criticism, philosophical reflections, and corporate statements. This expansion is often described as a democratization of knowledge. Yet the more fundamental condition is not simply the growth of knowledge, but the flooding of the world with knowledge-like text: text that appears coherent, scholarly, ethical, literary, or professional while often remaining generatively inert. This paper proposes a new theory of knowledge evaluation for this condition. It argues that the central challenge of the AI age is no longer only how to detect misinformation, hallucinated citations, plagiarism, or low-quality output. These problems matter, but they remain largely at the level of textual reliability. The deeper problem is how to distinguish living knowledge from hollow textual structure. Drawing on the Universal Phase Crystallization Theory, the paper defines textual structures, concepts, genres, academic formats, policy vocabularies, ethical principles, and disciplinary frameworks as S: provisional crystallizations of generative resonance. S is necessary for knowledge to be shared, stabilized, cited, criticized, and institutionalized. However, when S becomes detached from ΦR, it becomes hollow: formally correct but no longer capable of opening new questions, relations, interpretations, or futures. The paper therefore redefines knowledge not as information contained in a text, but as the dynamic process by which a text increases generative possibility Φ and relational resonance R over time. A text is alive when it continues to generate questions, reopen relations, renew interpretation, invite further inquiry, and transform readers, fields, institutions, or practices. A text is dead when it merely reproduces existing S, even if it remains correct, professional, safe, and institutionally recognizable. The central evaluative criterion is d(ΦR)/dt ≥ 0. This formula functions as a life-detection principle for knowledge. It asks whether a text sustains, increases, or reopens generative resonance over time. The paper applies this criterion to academic papers, literature, criticism, interdisciplinary knowledge, innovation protection, AI ethics, peer review, and information overload. Finally, the paper proposes a two-layer model of AI-based essential extraction. The first layer, S-verification, checks accuracy, citation validity, logic, method, redundancy, and hollow formulaic structure. The second layer, ΦR-detection, identifies whether a text opens new questions, exposes conceptual limits, creates interdisciplinary relations, preserves uncrystallized innovation, and sustains future generativity. AI should not become the final judge of knowledge; rather, it should become a mediator that helps human communities detect living knowledge within textual overproduction. Highlights The paper reframes AI-generated textual abundance as knowledge flooding, not merely information growth. It distinguishes living knowledge from hollow S, arguing that formally correct texts may still be generatively dead. It introduces d(ΦR)/dt ≥ 0 as a criterion for detecting whether a text increases generative possibility and relational resonance over time. It proposes a two-layer model of AI-based essential extraction: S-verification and ΦR-detection. It extends AI ethics from static safety, fairness, and transparency toward an ethics of intellectual filtering and generative resonance. It argues that AI should not simply generate, summarize, rank, or verify text, but help identify which texts remain alive as knowledge. It protects innovative knowledge by distinguishing fragile, uncrystallized Φ from low-quality noise. Contribution to AI and Generative Text Studies This paper contributes to AI and generative text studies by shifting the central concern from whether AI-generated text is accurate or human-like to whether it is generatively alive. Existing discussions often focus on hallucination, plagiarism, authorship, automation, labor displacement, and quality control. This paper does not reject those concerns, but argues that they do not fully capture the deeper epistemic transformation caused by generative AI. The paper introduces the concept of knowledge flooding to describe a condition in which AI produces not only more information, but more text that resembles knowledge. In such a condition, the main challenge is not simply output quality, but essential extraction: identifying which texts actually open new questions, relations, and futures. The paper also offers a new role for AI. Rather than treating AI only as a generator of text, it reconceives AI as a mediator of textual life. AI should assist in detecting redundancy, hollow form, citation failure, and unreliable S, while also surfacing fragile but potentially generative Φ. This reframing offers a more sophisticated account of AI’s role in future knowledge systems. Contribution to Epistemology This paper contributes to epistemology by redefining knowledge beyond the possession of justified information, propositions, concepts, or evidence. It argues that in the age of textual overproduction, knowledge must be understood not only as correctness or representation, but as temporal generativity. The paper proposes that knowledge becomes knowledge in the strong sense when a text mediates the increase of ΦR over time. This means that knowledge is not merely what a text contains, but what a text makes possible. A correct text may be epistemically inert, while an incomplete text may be epistemically alive if it opens a new field of inquiry. This reframing introduces a dynamic, relational, and temporal dimension into epistemology. It treats knowledge not as a static object but as a living process among text, reader, field, institution, history, and future. Contribution to Hermeneutics and Literary Theory This paper contributes to hermeneutics and literary theory by redefining textual value through resonance rather than extractable meaning alone. It argues that the essence of literature cannot be reduced to plot, theme, genre, symbolism, structure, or stylistic classification. These are analyzable forms of S, but they do not exhaust literary life. The paper proposes that literary ΦR appears as after-resonance, rereadability, affective transformation, renewed interpretation, and the disclosure of experience that had not yet found language. A literary work is alive when it continues to generate meaning and relation after reading. This framework offers a way to evaluate literature in the age of AI-generated imitation. AI can imitate genre, style, ambiguity, and emotional tone, but resemblance to literary S does not guarantee literary life. The decisive question becomes whether the text continues to generate ΦR within readers, communities, and historical reception. Contribution to Philosophy of Science and Peer Review Studies This paper contributes to philosophy of science and peer review studies by distinguishing between formal academic validity and living scholarly contribution. It argues that citation, method, literature review, disciplinary fit, and logical coherence are necessary but insufficient for detecting the essence of a paper. A scholarly paper becomes living knowledge when it changes what can be asked, tested, connected, challenged, or developed. The paper’s essence lies not merely in its conclusion, but in the future inquiry opened by that conclusion. The proposed two-layer model of AI-assisted review clarifies this distinction. S-verification supports conventional review by checking citation validity, method, redundancy, logic, and factual reliability. ΦR-detection adds a second evaluative layer by asking whether the paper opens new questions, exposes conceptual limits, creates interdisciplinary connections, or generates future research. This model could help prevent AI-assisted peer review from becoming a conservative mechanism that merely reinforces existing academic S. Contribution to Information Overload Studies This paper contributes to information overload studies by arguing that the contemporary problem is not simply too much information, but too much knowledge-like textual S. The challenge is not only cognitive overload, attention scarcity, or filtering efficiency. It is the difficulty of distinguishing living knowledge from formally coherent but generatively hollow text. The paper updates classical concerns about information overload for the generative AI age. When AI can produce academic, literary, ethical, policy, and explanatory text at scale, the bottleneck shifts from access to discernment. The main problem becomes not how to obtain information, but how to identify which textual structures remain connected to ΦR. The concept of essential extraction offers a new vocabulary for this condition. It suggests that the future of information systems should not be organized only around relevance, ranking, personalization, or summarization, but around the detection of textual life. Contribution to AI Ethics This paper contributes to AI ethics by expanding the field from static safety and bias reduction toward an ethics of intellectual filtering. Existing AI ethics emphasizes fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, safety, reliability, and harm prevention. These remain indispensable.
