# How to Design an Emotion: The Deconstruction and Computation of Emotional Meaning from First Principles

**著者**: Cézjah
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 哲学

## Abstract

What if the way you understand your own emotions is structurally wrong? Your heart races, your stomach tightens, and fear takes hold. But what actually occurred? Did your body undergo physical affect? Did you consciously experience a feeling? Or did you interpret raw sensation to construct emotional meaning? We build relationships, exercise agency, and execute moral judgments through emotion. We measure it, diagnose it, and program artificial intelligence to recognize it. Yet psychology, neuroscience, and philosophy still cannot agree on what an emotion actually is. How to Design an Emotion begins with that failure and confronts a deeper problem: how the human mind transforms raw experience into meaning, meaning into value, and value into action. Cézjah argues that our confusion stems from collapsing three distinct phenomena into one: affect is what the body undergoes, feeling is what a conscious agent experiences, and emotion is how the mind interprets that experience to yield meaning. Drawing on philosophy, affective neuroscience, cognitive psychology, semiotics, music theory, and computer science, Cézjah constructs a unified architecture across six foundational pillars: Identity & Meaning: How concepts preserve precise identity while allowing rich variation across contexts. Semiotic Construction: How an interpreting agent transforms private feeling into communicable meaning. Affective Translation: The exact mechanism through which a sovereign mind organizes physiological affect into structured emotion. Formal Coherence: A computational model testing whether emotional architecture survives executable logic. Cognitive Thresholds: The precise line where automatic appraisal ends and intentional interpretation begins. Agentic Governance: The integration of affect, feeling, and reason into a framework for rational action directed toward human flourishing and the prevention of harm. This is not another catalog of feelings, another self-help guide for regulation, or another claim that biology alone has solved the mystery. It is a systematic reconstruction from first principles. What do you feel? What does it mean? What will you do about it? The ultimate question is not simply whether humans experience emotions, but whether we understand them well enough to govern the lives we build, prevent harm, pursue well-being, and flourish.
