# The Sovereign Minds: Guarding Human Agency in the Age of Autonomous Machines

**著者**: Michael James Arnold
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 哲学

## Abstract

Description The Sovereign Minds: Guarding Human Agency in the Age of Autonomous Machines is a companion volume to Michael James Arnold's On Artificial Consciousness and the Future of Shared Cognition: Theoretical Foundations, Empirical Limits, and the Ethics of Human–AI Symbiosis. Together, the two works examine complementary dimensions of the emerging relationship between human and synthetic intelligence. While On Artificial Consciousness and the Future of Shared Cognition investigates artificial consciousness, human–AI symbiosis, and the possibilities of shared cognitive systems, The Sovereign Minds addresses the corresponding question of human autonomy: if cognition increasingly becomes shared between humans and machines, how do we ensure that collaboration does not become surrender? As artificial intelligence develops from an assistive technology into increasingly autonomous and agentic systems capable of reasoning, recommending, generating, coordinating, and acting on human behalf, the preservation of human agency becomes a central technological and social challenge. The Sovereign Minds examines how individuals and institutions can benefit from increasingly capable AI while preserving the human ability to think independently, exercise judgment, make meaningful choices, and retain responsibility for consequential decisions. Drawing together psychology, neuroscience, philosophy, artificial intelligence, law, education, political economy, institutional governance, and socio-technical systems, Michael James Arnold explores cognitive offloading, neuroplasticity, metacognition, algorithmic governance, multi-agent systems, AI alignment, surveillance, automated bureaucracy, autonomous warfare, algorithmic management, epistemic integrity, education, and the growing competition for human attention. The book is not an argument against artificial intelligence. Instead, it develops a framework for high-agency human–AI collaboration in which computational systems amplify human capability without quietly replacing human judgment. Its central concern is therefore not merely whether autonomous machines will become more capable, but whether the human beings who increasingly depend upon them will preserve their own cognitive competence and decision-making authority. This distinction is explicitly established in the book's preface, which describes the work as a defense of human sovereignty rather than an anti-technology manifesto. Across twenty chapters, The Sovereign Minds moves from individual cognitive autonomy through the architecture of autonomous systems and into the social, legal, cultural, educational, economic, and institutional consequences of algorithmic decision-making. It develops practical concepts including mindful friction, cognitive resilience, meaningful human oversight, human veto authority, attention autonomy, data sovereignty, algorithmic accountability, and the preservation of meaningful human judgment. The volume culminates in The Manifesto for Human Sovereignty and an extensive collection of appendices that translate the book's central principles into proposed technical, educational, legal, privacy, auditing, governance, and institutional frameworks.
