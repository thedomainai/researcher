# From Scientific Method to Architectures of Inquiry in the Age of AI

**著者**: David (Daoud) Matta
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 哲学

## Abstract

Artificial intelligence increasingly participates in activities that have traditionally constituted scientific inquiry, including literature search, pattern detection, hypothesis generation, modelling, simulation, experimental design, data analysis, criticism, and interpretation. Much of the emerging literature consequently asks whether AI is becoming a scientific agent, whether scientific discovery can be automated, or whether an identifiable AI method is emerging alongside established scientific methods. This paper argues that these questions, although important, do not capture the deeper methodological transformation now under way. As epistemic functions become modular, distributable, and recombinable across human researchers, artificial agents, models, databases, instruments, simulations, laboratories, and institutions, the central methodological problem shifts from the execution or selection of individual methods toward the organization of inquiry itself. The paper introduces architecture of inquiry as a higher-order methodological category: the epistemically governed configuration through which methods, agents, evidence, and validation processes are organized in the production of reliable knowledge. Six constitutive functions are derived across three dimensions: constitution (selection and allocation), process (sequencing and integration), and epistemic governance (validation and responsibility). The paper further proposes an Architectural Principle of Inquiry, according to which the epistemic reliability of a distributed inquiry system is not reducible to the reliability of its individual components but depends also upon the structure of the relations among them. Six relational properties through which that dependence operates are specified, and the principle is connected to formal results in social epistemology showing that individually rational agents need not compose into collectively reliable systems. The argument extends methodological pluralism, distributed cognition, and contemporary AI-for-science research, while remaining distinct from computational notions of multi-agent architecture. Recent empirical evidence that AI adoption expands individual scientific impact while contracting the collective breadth of scientific attention is interpreted here as an architectural finding rather than a merely sociological one. AI therefore does not mark the end of scientific method. It makes visible a methodological level above individual method: the deliberate architecture and governance of inquiry, together with the question of how far that architecture can itself be delegated.
