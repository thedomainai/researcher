# Toward a Philosophy of Interaction: How Designed Interaction Constitutes the Human, and Why Its Reversibility Now Matters

**著者**: Meng-Han Lee
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: HCI

## Abstract

“Interaction” is the word that names the field of human–computer interaction (HCI), yet the field has never settled what interaction, at bottom, does to the human who lives through it. HCI asks what an interaction affords, how it mediates, whether it is usable and trusted; this paper asks what those questions do not reach: how designed interaction constitutes the human, how sustained engagement with a designed interactional environment forms, in part, a person’s agency, self-understanding, embodied capacity, meaning-making, and responsibility. The question has drifted at the edges of several literatures without a home of its own; the agentic turn now makes it urgent. The urgency has a precise shape. Under human-in-the-loop arrangements, responsibility is increasingly assigned to the human: the system acts, the person answers for it. Yet a prior question is seldom faced, and never at the site where it arises: is that person still capable of answering, or is the very capacity responsibility presumes being eroded by the interaction itself? Governance, ethics, and regulation circle the problem from outside the design site, asking how to require responsibility of the human, never how the interaction forms or deforms the human who must bear it. The question belongs to HCI, and it carries an edge responsible design has had no way to name: is such formation reversible, can the person come to see it, take it back, undo it? The paper offers a lens, pressed out of design practice, and a way to use it: constitutive-of-being interaction as an analytical axis, shifting the unit of analysis from how interaction is construed or mediated to what it constitutes; three tenses of design (the designed, the designable, the post-designed); and three operative questions, where control over a person’s formation lies, into what the person is being constituted, and whether that constitution is reversible. Re-readings of existing empirical work, including studies with no AI and no responsibility at stake, show the constitution of the human to be older and wider than the agentic case: responsibility is where the question first becomes pressing, not the whole of what the lens sees. What the paper finally proposes is not that HCI import philosophers, but that it can grow, from its own practice, the conceptual capacity to ask such questions; this is the first tool.
