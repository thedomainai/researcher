# When the Backup Takes a Decade: AI Dependency, Active Human Fallback, and Hysteresis in Professional Formation

**著者**: John F. Ryder
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 人的資源管理

## Abstract

This paper examines professional AI substitution as a dependency and resilience problem rather than primarily as an employment problem. Its central argument is that human professional expertise represents an unusually slow-recovering fallback system. Senior capability is produced through formation pathways measured in years, while the experienced practitioners being depleted are themselves an input into producing their successors. The paper therefore distinguishes between a current-competence reserve—people capable of executing essential functions independently of the AI layer today—and a formation reserve—the entry, supervision, practice and teaching capacity required to reproduce that competence in future cohorts. Retention alone is not sufficient. Sustained AI mediation can erode independent capability even while human headcount remains stable. At the same time, correlated reductions in junior recruitment and supervised practice can create a trainer bottleneck in which renewed demand or higher wages cannot restore experienced capability on the timescale on which it was lost. Drawing on reliability engineering, business-continuity practice, human-factors research and earlier work on premature dependency and active fallback maintenance, the paper introduces two applied tests: an Operational Fallback Gate, concerned with whether independently executable human capability survives credible AI or infrastructure failure; and a Formation Recovery Gate, concerned with whether depleted capability can be regenerated before its absence becomes intolerable. The gates themselves are not claimed as novel. The contribution is the regenerative-latency asymmetry of human fallback: unlike most technological redundancy, professional expertise may take years to reconstruct, and its reconstruction rate may itself be constrained by the surviving stock of experienced practitioners. The resulting policy question is not simply how many jobs AI may remove, but how far AI dependency can safely advance before human capability becomes either unavailable in degraded operation or too slow to regenerate.
