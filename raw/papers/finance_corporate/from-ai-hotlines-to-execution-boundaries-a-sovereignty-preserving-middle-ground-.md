# From AI Hotlines to Execution Boundaries: A Sovereignty-Preserving Middle Ground for U.S.–China AI Safety

**著者**: Sangam Das
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0

## Abstract

A Technical Proposal for Preventing Unauthorized AI Effectuation Without Requiring Mutual Trust in Models Abstract The emerging U.S.–China dialogue on artificial-intelligence safety creates an unusual area of possible technical convergence between two countries that otherwise remain strategic competitors in advanced AI. In September 2026, U.S. and Chinese officials held discussions on AI-related issues, and U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent subsequently described plans for a formalized bilateral AI dialogue, including an “incident line” for communication regarding serious AI safety events and future discussion of risks including uncontrollable agents and malicious non-state actors. Chinese official reporting separately confirmed that AI-related issues were discussed. The more difficult question is what can be done before an incident occurs. This paper proposes execution-finality architecture as one possible technical middle layer. The central principle is simple: An AI system may calculate, recommend, authorize, request or prepare an action, but computation alone does not give that action authority to become effective. Under an execution-finality architecture, a proposed action remains non-effective until a component controlling the actual consequence independently verifies the exact action against current protected authorization and system state. Only after successful verification may the consequence become effective. This approach could permit cooperation without requiring the United States to trust Chinese models, China to trust American models, or either side to disclose model weights, training data, internal reasoning or proprietary AI architectures. The proposal is therefore not a model-governance treaty. It is a narrower technical proposition: different sovereign systems can retain independent final authority over the consequences produced by AI systems, even when the originating AI comes from another jurisdiction, company or trust domain. The Emerging Common Problem The United States and China have substantially different strategic interests in artificial intelligence. Competition over advanced semiconductors, frontier models, intellectual property, infrastructure, standards and national-security applications is unlikely to disappear merely because both governments recognize AI safety risks. Yet there is an increasingly important distinction between: competing over who develops the most capable AI and allowing an AI system to cause an uncontrolled real-world consequence. That second problem can affect both sides. The United States has formally required AI adopted within its national-security enterprise to be designed to be reliable, robust, steerable and controllable, with clear accountability and verification requirements. China's 2026 World Artificial Intelligence Conference statement similarly called for AI to remain under human control and specifically stated that AI agents should operate with clearly defined decision-making authority and behavioral boundaries, together with behavior-tracing and risk-alert mechanisms. It also identified risks to critical infrastructure including finance, electricity, telecommunications and transportation. China has additionally issued national policy concerning AI agents capable of autonomous perception, memory, decision-making, interaction and execution, emphasizing safety, controllability and management of agent behavior throughout deployment. These policies are not identical. They arise from different political, legal and strategic systems. But technically they expose a common engineering question: When an autonomous AI decides to do something, what prevents that decision from automatically becoming the real-world consequence? 2. An Incident Hotline Is Important — But It Operates Late The proposed U.S.–China AI incident communication mechanism could be valuable. Its basic logic is: AI incident occurs or is detected→ responsible authorities identify a serious risk→ governments communicate→ escalation or further damage may be managed. Such mechanisms can reduce misunderstanding and provide channels for emergency coordination. But an incident line is primarily a communication mechanism. It does not itself determine whether an AI command should be permitted to: transfer funds; SEND a confidential file or message to an external destination; issue a command to electrical infrastructure; change a telecommunications configuration; invoke a military or civilian autonomous system; alter a critical database; release protected information; execute cloud-administration operations; control industrial machinery; instruct another autonomous agent; or initiate an irreversible cyber operation. These actions occur at technical execution boundaries. A complementary question therefore becomes: Can some dangerous AI actions be stopped at the boundary before an international incident needs to be reported? 3. From AI Safety to Execution Safety Much AI-safety work concentrates on the model. Important approaches include: alignment; model evaluation; red teaming; prompt security; policy filters; access control; authentication; sandboxing; model monitoring; explainability; human oversight; behavioral restrictions. All are potentially valuable. But there is another architectural layer. Consider an autonomous agent that has already: authenticated successfully; received valid credentials; passed an upstream policy check; generated a legitimate-looking request; obtained authorization to use a tool. The remaining question is: Is the exact operation that is now about to happen still the operation that was actually permitted? Authorization and effectuation are related but not identical. A valid credential might authorize an agent generally while the actual execution could nevertheless be: stale; replayed; duplicated; substituted; redirected; performed after revocation; executed against changed system state; expanded beyond its original scope; or altered between authorization and execution. Execution-finality architecture addresses this last boundary. 4. The Execution-Finality Principle An execution-finality architecture keeps a proposed action in a non-effective state until the component controlling the actual consequence independently determines that the precise effect about to occur remains permitted. Conceptually: AI MODEL / AGENT | | proposes an action v AUTHORIZATION / POLICY / IDENTITY | | candidate operation v NON-EFFECTIVE STATE | v +--------------------------------+ | FINAL EFFECTUATION BOUNDARY | | | | Reconstruct exact actual act | | Verify authorization binding | | Verify current protected state | | Verify freshness | | Verify replay state | | Verify revocation/generation | | Verify exact destination | | Verify permitted parameters | +--------------------------------+ | +------ verification fails ------> NO EFFECT | +------ verification succeeds ---> ATOMIC EFFECT The enforcement component at this location can be described functionally as a Finality Sink. The terminology is less important than the function. In conventional engineering language, similar functions may be described as a: reference monitor; policy enforcement point; command gate; actuation interlock; transaction-validation boundary; execution gateway; safety interlock; secure monitor; effectuation-control point; trusted commit boundary. What distinguishes execution finality is the requirement that verification applies to the actual consequence about to become effective, using current protected state, and that successful verification is coupled to the resulting transition. 5. Why This Could Form a Sovereignty-Preserving Middle Ground The central advantage for international cooperation is that execution finality does not necessarily require one country to trust the other's AI. Imagine an American AI agent interacting with infrastructure governed by China. The architecture could be: American AI | | proposes operation v Chinese-controlled execution boundary | | verifies exact requested consequence | under Chinese-local protected rules v ALLOW -----------------> consequence occurs DENY -----------------> no consequence The reverse relationship could operate identically: Chinese AI | | proposes operation v American-controlled execution boundary | | verifies exact requested consequence | under U.S.-local protected rules v ALLOW -----------------> consequence occurs DENY -----------------> no consequence Neither side must make the assertion: “Trust our AI.” Instead, each side can retain the narrower position: “Your AI may request an action. Our infrastructure retains final authority over whether that action becomes effective.” This is fundamentally different. The model can remain foreign. The model can remain proprietary. The model can remain opaque. The model owner does not necessarily need to disclose: model weights; source code; training datasets; chain-of-thought or internal reasoning; proprietary inference architecture; internal safety systems; strategic intellectual property.
