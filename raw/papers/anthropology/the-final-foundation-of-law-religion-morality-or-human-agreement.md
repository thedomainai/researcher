# The Final Foundation of Law: Religion, Morality or Human Agreement?

## Abstract

The Final Foundation of Law: Religion, Morality or Human Agreement? challenges one of jurisprudence's oldest and most fundamental questions: Why do human beings obey the law? If modern legal systems no longer derive their authority from divine command or objective moral order, what remains to sustain the experience of legal obligation? Through an original interdisciplinary inquiry, Northon Salomão de Oliveira argues that contemporary law continues to rely on what he calls "borrowed sanctity": the inherited rituals, symbols, and institutional forms that once belonged to religious authority but now silently underpin secular legal systems. Courtrooms, judicial ceremonies, constitutions, legal traditions, and even emerging systems of artificial intelligence preserve a grammar of legitimacy that survives long after its theological foundations have faded. Engaging critically with the works of H.L.A. Hart, Hans Kelsen, Ronald Dworkin, Lon Fuller, David Hume, and other major legal philosophers, the book develops a provocative thesis: law does not ultimately stand upon religion, morality, or pure social agreement alone. Instead, its authority emerges from a complex interplay of ritual, inheritance, collective trust, and institutional continuity that enables societies to coordinate behavior across generations. Extending its analysis to algorithmic governance, artificial intelligence, digital evidence, constitutional interpretation, and climate justice, The Final Foundation of Law offers a fresh framework for understanding legal legitimacy in the twenty-first century. Rather than searching for an unquestionable metaphysical foundation, it proposes a jurisprudence centered on the responsible stewardship of the social mechanisms that generate trust, authority, and obedience. Philosophically ambitious yet grounded in contemporary legal challenges, this book invites scholars, jurists, policymakers, and anyone interested in the future of law to reconsider the invisible architecture upon which every legal order ultimately depends. 1.
