# Haji Bushra of Gata and the making of Islamic education in nineteenth-century South Wollo, Ethiopia: institutional formation and community agency

**著者**: Amanuel Kidane Naizgi, Wubu Desale Fentaw
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 人類学

## Abstract

Abstract Scholarship on Islam in Ethiopia has examined religious authority, scholarly networks, and Muslim communities, but has paid less attention to how local centres of Islamic learning were established and sustained. This article examines Gata Majlis in nineteenth-century South Wollo, focusing on how Haji Bushra’s scholarly authority shaped its formation, how learning continued after his death, and how later generations remembered his role. It also considers the centre within the social and political context of nineteenth-century Wollo and distinguishes its institutional history from later traditions of religious memory and pilgrimage. The study draws on 20 manuscripts, institutional and historical records, 12 semi-structured interviews, two focus-group discussions, and published scholarship. Eight manuscripts were selected for detailed analysis based on provenance, relevance, and textual accessibility, while twelve provided contextual evidence. Historical criticism, textual and thematic analysis examined institutional development, knowledge transmission, community participation, and historical memory. The findings indicate that Haji Bushra was central to Gata Majlis’s formation, but its continuity depended on teachers, students, custodians, families, and local supporters, who sustained instruction, preserved manuscripts, provided material support, and maintained community relationships. The surviving manuscripts provide stronger evidence of Gata’s intellectual tradition and its transmission over time. The article contributes to Ethiopian Islamic historiography by showing how Islamic learning was sustained through community participation, educational practices, and manuscript preservation beyond the lifetime of a prominent scholar. Gata Majlis offers a close example of why the history of Islamic education must account not only for influential scholars but also for the people and practices that carried their traditions across generations.
