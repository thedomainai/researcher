# WHICH MASTER FREES? Idolatry as Occupation — The Golden Calf, the Pierced Ear and the Empty Seat: Why Submission to What Needs Nothing Cannot Enslave, and Why No One May Sit in Its Place

**著者**: José Caetano de Mattos
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 宗教学

## Abstract

Abstract Freedom and virtue are not modern inventions imposed on religion from outside. The world’s religions have been, for three thousand years, among their most careful guardians: they taught that a good act must come from the person who does it, that faith compelled is not faith, and that no human being may stand where the ultimate should be. Every tradition that has thought seriously about freedom has had to answer a question that looks like a contradiction. The Torah says that Israel was freed from Egypt in order to serve God; the Mishnah says that no one is free except the one who takes up the Law; Augustine says that true freedom is service to God; Islam is named for submission and teaches that it frees from every other servitude. Modern readers, and much of the programme’s own comparative material, have read these claims as the opposite of freedom. This article argues that they are a precise theory of it. The argument rests on the programme’s deprivation operator, which removes an agent’s freedom and delivers the form of his virtue to the power that removed it. A submission can therefore enslave in only two ways: by delivering something to the master itself, or by delivering it to whoever speaks in the master’s name. From this follow the article’s two central results. A master that needs nothing cannot be the receiving power of the operator: what is given to it is received by no one (T1). And submission frees if and only if its object neither receives anything nor has a seat that another can occupy (T2). For the God of classical theism the first condition holds by definition — the Psalms, Acts, the Qurʾān’s divine name al-Ghanī and Aquinas all say that God needs nothing and is honoured for the worshipper’s sake — so the entire moral and political weight of religious submission falls on the second. God needs nothing; the only one who can profit from a person’s submission to God is whoever stands in God’s place. Idolatry is then the theological name of the occupation operator (T3). The golden calf keeps the form of worship, keeps the name of the God who brought Israel out of Egypt — Aaron proclaims “a feast to the LORD” — replaces the source with a made thing, and sends the gold to its makers. Every tradition that names an unoccupiable master builds rules that close the seat to human occupants: the ban on images, the hidden grave of Moses, the pierced ear of the Hebrew slave who chooses a human master, “call no man your father on earth”, the refusal of obedience to any creature against the Creator, the Buddha’s refusal to name a successor, and the Sikh Gurus’ transfer of guruship to a book (T4). By the traditions’ own criterion, a human ruler who claims the seat of the unoccupiable master is the paradigm idol, and the more faithfully he imitates the true God’s voice the greater the harm (T5). The result decides the metaphysical fork the programme left open. Free choice cannot be what makes a submission free — the Hebrew servant chose his human master freely, and the Talmud condemns him — so the difference must lie in the object, and the property that makes it is self-sufficiency. The participative branch, on which creaturely freedom is received from an aseity that needs nothing, supplies it; the rival branch, on which freedom is uncreated and prior to God, must either borrow it or, with Berdyaev, keep a God who needs man and lose the distinction altogether (T6). For the secular reader the same theorem has a political form older than modern democracy: since nothing in the world needs nothing, no worldly master may receive total submission, and the seat of sovereignty must remain empty (T7). The practical rule is simple: to find an idol, follow the receipt — ask who collects what is given to it. A leader cult, a party, a market treated as destiny, a state honour roll such as China’s Social Credit System, and an AI system trusted as an oracle each have a seat, and someone sits in it. An appendix applies the theorem to coercion of faith: since God needs nothing, any coercion of belief in God’s name — the classical jizya and apostasy laws of Islamic jurisprudence, the forced baptisms and heresy trials of Christendom, the death penalties of Deuteronomy — has a human recipient, and is proof that the seat has been occupied. Priority is conceded to Tillich, Luther, Calvin, Halbertal and Margalit, Fromm, Augustine, Aquinas, Ibn Taymiyya, Lefort, Voegelin and Weil, among others. Three tests are registered; none has been run. Freedom and virtue survive only where no human power is allowed to stand where the ultimate should be. Relevance The question of which master to obey is not antiquarian. Every generation hands its trust to something — a nation, a party, a leader, a market, a science, a platform, and now an artificial intelligence — and every such master is spoken for by someone. The traditions that have most to say about freedom spent three thousand years building a single safeguard against this: the rule that the highest authority must have no human occupant. Modern constitutional democracy inherited the rule in secular form, as the principle that no person or party may embody the people. The safeguard is eroding. Leaders present themselves as the voice of the nation; parties claim to be the conscience of history; states that score their citizens’ virtue, as China’s Social Credit System does, install a scorer in the place where conscience should be; and systems trained to please their users are increasingly consulted as if they were neutral oracles, while someone controls what they say. This article gives a formal criterion for telling a master that frees from a master that enslaves, shows that the world’s traditions converge on it, and translates it into rules for persons, religious communities, states and the designers of AI. It also shows that the religions, read by their own deepest texts, are allies of freedom and virtue rather than their rivals: they judge every act by whether it comes from the person who does it, they hold that faith cannot be compelled, and they forbid any human being to stand in the place of the ultimate. And it gives believers and non-believers a common ground that does not require them to agree about God: whether or not God exists, no one may sit in God’s place.
