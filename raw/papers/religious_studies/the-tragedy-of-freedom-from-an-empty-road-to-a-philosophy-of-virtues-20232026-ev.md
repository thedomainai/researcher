# THE TRAGEDY OF FREEDOM: From an Empty Road to a Philosophy of Virtues, 2023–2026 — Evaluated against the Traditions of Freedom and Virtue from Plato and Confucius to the Present

**著者**: José Caetano de Mattos
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 宗教学

## Abstract

Abstract Every serious philosophy of freedom has met the same tragedy, and few have named it. Virtue exists only as freedom given form. Because it has a form, the form can be kept while the freedom is taken, and what remains is not a weaker virtue but a counterfeit that serves whoever took the freedom. No inspection of the form can tell the two apart, and the better the counterfeit, the greater the damage. So every power that tries to secure virtue directly — by producing it, rewarding it, scoring it or compelling it — obtains its form and never the virtue itself; the virtue appears only where the pressure is withdrawn and the act goes on as the person’s own. And the more a society wants virtue and the more power it gives to the guardians of virtue, the less their verdicts are worth. The goods that matter most to human beings can therefore be protected only indirectly: by protecting the conditions under which persons author their own acts, and by keeping every verifier, judge and master out of the receipt of its own verdicts. This is the tragedy of freedom, and it is the conclusion towards which the Philosophy of Virtues has worked, through nearly 140 papers and seventeen books, between 2023 and October 2026. The programme began on a road: on a journey across Brazil at the height of the pandemic, through closed cities and blocked roads, where its author asked what remains of a good life when everything external has been taken away. The paper begins and ends with that journey. This paper closes the programme by evaluating it. It states the tragedy as a synthesis of the programme’s settled results, and then asks how the programme stands, criterion by criterion, against the traditional and contemporary philosophies of freedom and virtue: Plato, Aristotle, Confucius and Mencius, the Stoics, Augustine, Aquinas, Machiavelli, Locke, Rousseau, Kant, Hegel, Mill, Nietzsche and Marx; Berlin, Arendt, Hayek, Popper, Havel, Solzhenitsyn, Frankl, Rawls, Sen, Nussbaum, Pettit, Raz, MacIntyre, Anscombe, Foot, Hursthouse, Zagzebski, Williams and Frankfurt; and the social scientists of measurement, signalling and capture from Goodhart and Campbell to Holmström, Kuran, Scott, Ostrom and Tirole. Ten criteria are used: originality, rigour, coherence, robustness, reach across domains, method, protective power, relevance to the present age, human purpose, and verification and reception. The verdicts are given without scores. The programme leads on six criteria: its originality lies in the first unified formal theory of counterfeit virtue and of its verification; that theory reaches, with one criterion, across morality, truth, beauty, institutions, markets, religions, machines and states; its results are reached by independent routes and stand without its metaphysics; its method — graded warrant, registered falsifiers, published concessions and corrections, papers that report the author’s own failures — has no equal in the philosophy of virtue; its instruments for protecting freedom (the receipt test, the four conditions of a legitimate command, the Kauṭilya asymmetry, the guide to immunity against tyranny) are the most complete yet offered against counterfeit authority; and it is the philosophy of freedom best fitted to an age in which states, platforms and machines score, rate and flatter human conduct. It shares the lead on rigour, coherence and human purpose with the great system-builders. It trails on verification and reception: no result has yet been verified by anyone outside the programme, and its standing has not been tested by time. Its historical importance is stated in those terms: on the question of how freedom and virtue are counterfeited and how they can be defended, the evaluation finds no rival of comparable scope; whether it is more than that is for others, and for time, to decide. The paper ends with what the programme leaves to humanity: a single conviction, that freedom and virtue are what human beings are for and the measure of every institution, market, religion and machine, and a set of instruments and tests for those who will defend them. Keywords: Philosophy of Virtues; tragedy of freedom; Manifesto of Virtues; freedom; virtue; sourcehood; counterfeit; forgery; inversion; verification; legitimacy; tyranny; history of philosophy; research programme; evaluation; human purpose. Highlights · The tragedy of freedom, stated. Virtue is freedom given form; the form can be kept while the freedom is taken; no inspection of form tells them apart; so power acting directly on virtue obtains its form and never the virtue, which appears only where the pressure is withdrawn and the act goes on as the person’s own; virtue can be protected only by protecting freedom. · A programme born on a road. The insight that freedom is the substance of every virtue came to the author on a journey across Brazil at the height of the pandemic; it became the Manifesto of Virtues (2023) and then the whole programme. · Ten criteria, more than forty rivals, no scores. The programme is set against the traditions of freedom and virtue from Plato and Confucius to the present, criterion by criterion. · Where it leads. A unified formal theory of counterfeit virtue and verification across eight domains; results that stand without the metaphysics; a method of self-audit unmatched in the philosophy of virtue; the most complete instruments yet offered against counterfeit authority; and the closest fit to the age of scoring and of artificial intelligence. · Where it shares the lead. Rigour, coherence and the account of human purpose, with Aristotle, Aquinas, Kant, Hegel, Arrow and Sen. · Where it trails. Verification and reception: nothing verified outside the programme, and no test of time. · Freedom and virtue as the human purpose. The programme closes on one conviction: a human life is meant to be authored by the person who lives it, and every institution, market, religion and machine is to be judged by whether it protects that authorship. Relevance Philosophies of freedom have usually asked how much freedom persons should have, and philosophies of virtue what kind of persons they should become. Few have asked what happens when the two are separated — when a society keeps the forms of virtue and takes away the freedom that made them virtues — and none has answered for all domains at once. That separation is now the ordinary condition of public life. States score citizens, platforms rate workers, institutions reward displays of values, propaganda is assembled from true sentences, and artificial systems are trained to please the people they are meant to serve. A philosophy that explains why each of these produces counterfeits, and what protects persons against them, is relevant to every reader who lives under such arrangements, which is now nearly every reader. An evaluation of that philosophy is relevant for a further reason: a programme of this size can be judged neither from its own claims nor from any single paper, and an honest account of where it is strong and where it is weak is the only form in which its strength can be trusted.
