## Abstract

Introduction This series of fifteen conversations examines the ideas of the Italian researcher Francesco Carotta. His theory is unusual: he argues that the story of Jesus in the Gospels may have developed from stories written about Julius Caesar—especially his assassination, funeral, and later worship as a divine figure. Carotta does not claim that early Christians deliberately invented Jesus. His idea is much more subtle. He suggests that stories about Caesar were told, translated, retold, and gradually placed in a Jewish setting. Over time, he argues, Caesar’s story changed into the Gospel story: Rome became Palestine, Latin names became Greek and Hebrew-sounding names, and the murdered Roman dictator became a suffering Jewish teacher and saviour. Some of Carotta’s best-known comparisons concern the death of Caesar and the death of Jesus. Caesar was stabbed in the Senate; his body was displayed to the people on a cross-shaped trophy; crowds mourned him; and he was soon honoured as a god. Carotta asks whether echoes of these events can be found in the Gospel accounts of Jesus’ trial, crucifixion, burial, and resurrection. The conversations also look at his use of artificial intelligence. Carotta uses AI not as proof of his theory, but as a research assistant: a way to search large amounts of ancient text for repeated names, wordplay, numerical patterns, manuscript variants, and narrative parallels. One example discussed in the series is the mysterious “Number of the Beast” in the Book of Revelation. Carotta argues that the Greek name Cleopatra may explain why ancient manuscripts give different numbers—616, 666, and 665—while older explanations usually focus on the Roman emperor Nero. These fifteen sessions invite the reader to look closely at his evidence, ask how strong each argument is, and consider whether an unconventional theory at first sight can still raise useful questions about how the Christian story came into being. carotta.de 24/10/2026 1. CHAT – DIE DREI MARIEN Examines the women at the cross and tomb, especially the “other Mary,” Maria hē tou Iakōbou (“Mary the mother of James”). The chat begins with Greek transliteration of Latin names, the development of Latin V into Greek ου or β, and the textual instability of names such as Iēsous, Iōsēs, and Iakōbos. It gives particular weight to the Old Syriac Sinaiticus Syriacus, which at Mark 15:40 and Matthew 27:56 identifies Mary not as the mother of James and Joses/Joseph, but as “Mary the mother of Jesus.” The later Peshitta, by contrast, returns to the Greek-style formulation “mother of James and Joseph.” Within the transposition model, these variants are not treated merely as later harmonization. They become evidence that the Gospel women may preserve overlapping Roman family identities, with “Mary” functioning as a coded marker for women of the Julian line rather than as a straightforward biographical identification. 2. CHAT – LE COLOMBINE Explores dove imagery as a symbolic bridge between Gospel narrative and Roman imperial religion. The discussion connects the dove not only with the Holy Spirit at Jesus’ baptism, but with the wider iconography of Venus, Aphrodite, Isis, and the divinized Caesar. A central move is to read the dove as one of Caesar’s “signs”: a political and religious emblem that could be transferred from the cultic world of Divus Iulius into the Johannine story. In this reading, the baptismal dove is less an isolated Christian symbol than a recoded imperial sign. The chat also extends the argument toward Revelation, where the woman clothed with the sun, the dragon, and the child caught up to God are read as a Ptolemaic-Isiac counter-narrative to Augustan propaganda. Cleopatra and Caesarion are thereby contrasted with Octavian, who appears as the dragon or beast. 3. CHAT – ΤΙC ΒΑCΙΛΙΚΟC / QVIDAM REGIVS Analyzes the “royal official” of John 4:46, Greek tis basilikos and Vulgate quidam regulus/regius. The chat distinguishes the standard reading—a high official of Herod Antipas—from the possibility that basilikos means a person of royal or imperial stock. It notes that some Greek witnesses and patristic interpreters use basiliskos, “little king,” corresponding to Latin regulus. Origen is cited as supporting the idea that the man could have belonged to Caesar’s imperial household rather than merely to Herod’s administration. Under the diegetic-transposition hypothesis, the episode is read as a disguised dynastic healing narrative: the sick son is associated with the young Octavian, and Caesar’s intervention becomes the hidden historical kernel behind Jesus’ healing of the official’s child. The chat links this to the absence of the story from Mark, which it interprets as Antonian censorship of Octavian’s early legitimacy. 4. CHAT – LA PERLE DE SERVILIA Centres on Servilia, the mother of Brutus and Caesar’s long-term lover, and on the “pearl” as a symbol of Caesar’s financial and emotional settlement with her. The pearl is read not simply as a luxury object but as a calculated indemnity, a public mark of Caesar’s power over Servilia’s dignity and family honour. The chat argues that Brutus’ later assassination of Caesar should be understood, in this recto-layer, as a reaction to that humiliation: Caesar’s clemency, offices, and gifts are interpreted as forms of purchase rather than genuine favour. The Ides of March thus become a violent attempt to free Brutus from moral and financial dependence. This material is then mapped onto Judas Iscariot. Judas’ bargaining over money, his betrayal for silver, and his subsequent despair are read as a compressed, transposed version of the Servilia-Brutus drama, with the thirty pieces of silver echoing Caesar’s “consolation payment.” 5. CHAT – DIE JÜNGER Reinterprets the disciples as transposed Roman political actors rather than as a simple Galilean follower group. A key proposal is that Mark represents an Antonian perspective: the note preserved by Ephrem that Mark wrote “in Latin from Simon in the city of Rome” is read as a coded reference to Marcus Antonius (ANTONIVS reversed as SVINOTNA → Simon). On this reading, Mark’s omission of the Lazarus and royal-official episodes is not accidental tradition-history, but deliberate political censorship. Antonius would have had no interest in preserving stories that legitimized the young Octavian as Caesar’s chosen heir. John then becomes the Augustan rejoinder: after Actium, the Johannine redactor reintroduces the basilikos and Lazarus miracles, restoring the dynastic bond between Caesar/Jesus and the young heir. The chat also identifies Simon Peter with Antonius and Andreas with Curio, reading John 1:40’s “they followed him” as referring to Jesus/Caesar rather than to John the Baptist. 6. CHAT – DIE AUFERWECKUNG DES LAZARUS Reads the raising of Lazarus as a transposition of Caesar’s restoration of the Marian faction after Sulla’s proscriptions. Plutarch’s account that Caesar restored the banned images and honours of Marius—bringing them, as it were, back from Hades—is treated as the historical substrate of John 11. Lazarus is identified with Marius, Caesar’s uncle and the politically “buried” Marian leader; Mary corresponds to Julia, Marius’ widow and Caesar’s aunt; and Martha corresponds to the Syrian prophetess who accompanied Marius. Martha’s confession of resurrection in John 11 thus reflects the seer’s role in Marius’ entourage. The subsequent anointing at Bethany is interpreted as the ritual aftermath of Julia’s funeral: Mary’s nard and Martha’s service become an apotropaic protection rite for Caesar, later reinterpreted as an anticipation of Jesus’ own burial. Judas’ objection over the cost of the ointment is read as a coded version of Optimates’ attacks on Caesar’s spending and land redistribution. 7. CHAT – IVLII > IVDAEI Develops the onomastic hypothesis that Greek Ioulioi/Iouliōn, “Julii / of the Julii,” could be visually confused with Ioudaioi/Ioudaiōn, “Judeans / Jews.” In majuscule script, the difference between lambda and delta is minimal, and the surrounding religious vocabulary made Ioudaioi the easier reading for copyists. The chat applies this to John 4:22, “salvation is from the Jews” (ex Ioudaiōn). In the transposition model, the original phrase is reconstructed as “salvation is from the Julii”: Caesar, and then Augustus, were proclaimed in the Greek East as Sōtēr, saviour, and as bringers of the Pax Romana. The AI initially resists the conjecture on conventional historical and theological grounds, but the dialogue reframes it: once Gallia becomes Galilee, Corfinium Capernaum, and Roma Jerusalem, the Julian formula becomes the ideological core later disguised as a Jewish salvation-historical claim. The chat also connects Iunius Brutus to Ioudas Judas, with Iskariot related to sicarius, “dagger-man.” 8. CHAT – REKAPITULATION Assembles the preceding chats into a single model of John as a layered political document. Its basic distinction is between a recto layer—a chronicle of Caesar’s civil-war career—and a verso layer—Augustan dynastic propaganda centred on Octavian’s adoption and rule. The prologue is reinterpreted accordingly: archē means “rule” or “imperium,” and logosmeans “dispute” or “legal conflict.” Thus John 1:1 originally reports a struggle for power; only the inserted verses 1:12–14 transform that conflict into the theological language of divine generation and incarnation. The recapitulation also presents the wider consequence: the New Testament is portrayed as a highly engineered political-religious text, which fulfilled Caesar’s amnesty decree by erasing his profane name behind sacred formulae such as IC and the Chi-Rho. Later literalist translation, especially beyond the Roman frontier, is said to have destroyed the bilingual codes that once made the Roman substrate visible. 9.
