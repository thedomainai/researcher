# Isomorphic Evolution of Law

**著者**: Spring water
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 法学

## Abstract

This is not a treatise on legal philosophy. It is a civilizational engineering manual disguised as jurisprudence. The Isomorphic and Correlative Evolution of Procedural and Substantive Justice in Law advances a foundational proposition that strikes at the bedrock of every legal system on Earth: procedural justice is not the partner of substantive justice—it is its instrument, its follower, its servant. When procedure severs itself from substance, the result is not merely injustice but civilizational collapse. The Qin dynasty fell not despite its perfect legal code but because of it. Rome declined not from barbarian invasion but from the sclerosis of its own procedural formalism. The French Ancien Régime collapsed under the weight of its own ornate procedural privilege. And the United States today is tearing itself apart through mechanisms that are procedurally impeccable yet substantively absurd—gerrymandering, the Electoral College, the War on Drugs, and the Epstein non-prosecution agreement, which demonstrates not judicial corruption but the structural capture of entire procedural apparatuses by extreme wealth. What distinguishes this work from conventional legal scholarship is its refusal to treat these as isolated scandals or cultural peculiarities. Instead, it identifies four interlocking pathological mechanisms—Cumulative Effect, Legitimacy Depletion, Feedback Failure, and Spillover & Cascade—that convert procedural rigidity into systemic cancer. These are not metaphors; they are mechanistic laws of institutional decay, demonstrated through comparative legal history across East and West, ancient and modern, from the lianzuo system of the Qin to the Law of Citations of late Rome, from the ba yi privileges of Ming-Qing China to the procedural theater of the French Estates-General. For the professional reader, the book offers something rare in jurisprudence: a fully actionable institutional architecture. The authors propose a complete governance ecosystem comprising three independent institutions—the Independent Fact-Finding and Investigation Committee (IFIC), the Independent Audit and Supervision Bureau (IASB), and the Global Correction and Recovery Fund (GCRF)—backed by a Global Clearing and Supervision Platform (GCSP) built on permissioned blockchain architecture, zero-knowledge proof compliance verification, and automated freeze/block triggers. Sunset clauses force every law to undergo periodic "field testing" rather than eternal performance. Fast-track correction channels convert legal amendment from political theater into operational maintenance. A quantified compensation model mandates that the economic damage of procedural alienation be measured, audited, and repaired through institutionalized fiscal transfer. The framework directly confronts twenty-first-century pathologies that existing legal theory cannot capture: algorithmic discrimination laundered through "procedural legitimacy," platform monopolies disguised as "contractual freedom," transnational tax avoidance enabled by treaty procedure, and climate delay engineered through international process. It demonstrates why China's 2006 Peng Yu case—where a judge asked, "If you didn't knock her down, why would you help her up?"—destroyed a nation's moral reflex to assist strangers; why the Simpson trial polarized American racial consciousness by substituting technical exoneration for substantive truth; and why the Epstein saga represents the ultimate flowering of procedural logic when permanently severed from substantive accountability. This is a book for jurists who have grown tired of watching law degenerate from guardian of order into incubator of violence. It is for institutional designers who need more than critique—they need blueprints. It is for judges, legislators, constitutional architects, and regulatory technologists who understand that the question is no longer whether procedural justice should evolve, but whether civilization can survive its refusal to do so. The text does not offer a blueprint for any particular society; it offers a compass for any intelligent civilization that wishes to govern itself through rules without self-destruction. If your work touches constitutional design, judicial reform, international governance, regulatory technology, or the philosophy of law in an age of algorithmic governance, this text is not supplementary reading. It is the foundational jurisprudential proposition upon which the next century of legal architecture must be built—if law is to remain law, and civilization is to remain civilization.
