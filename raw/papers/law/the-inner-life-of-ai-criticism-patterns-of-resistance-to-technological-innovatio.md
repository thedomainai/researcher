# The Inner Life of AI Criticism: Patterns of Resistance to Technological Innovation and the Need for Academic Metacognition

**著者**: Nagae Mamoru
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 法学

## Abstract

This paper does not place the capacities or dangers of generative AI at the center of its inquiry. Rather, it asks what is at work within academia when academia criticizes generative AI. Generative AI has clear limitations. It can fabricate references, produce plausible falsehoods, reproduce dominant assumptions, and confuse fluency with understanding. Verification, methodological control, and responsible use are therefore indispensable. This paper does not dismiss criticism of AI itself. The problem is that such criticism does not always arise solely from the pursuit of truth. Technological innovation almost always generates resistance when it unsettles existing forms of authority, institutions, and interest structures. Yet historically, technological innovation has rarely, if ever, been stopped by resistance itself. The crucial question, therefore, is not whether generative AI can simply be rejected, but what such resistance is trying to protect: the conditions of truth-seeking, or the status, credentials, authority, habitual procedures, and existing boundaries of participation that have governed academic legitimacy. When academia criticizes AI without examining its own inner disposition, that criticism becomes risky. On the surface, it may invoke legitimate concerns: misinformation, plagiarism, declining intellectual discipline, research ethics, and the burden placed on peer review. Yet beneath these concerns, institutional self-defense may also be operating: a desire to control who is permitted to produce knowledge. In such cases, criticism of AI may appear to defend truth-seeking while actually distancing academia from the original vocation of scholarship. Scholarship is not the preservation of authority, but the continual opening of inquiry toward truth. Knowledge is not the property of any institution or professional class; it is a public process formed through evidence, verification, criticism, and revision. When evaluation shifts from evidence and testability toward affiliation, form, and procedural purity, academia risks becoming less a guardian of knowledge than a manager of access to knowledge. The consequence is not merely the exclusion of low-quality intellectual output. Immature but valuable questions, unfamiliar intuitions from outside institutions, and hypotheses not yet legible within existing frameworks may also be prematurely dismissed. Far from protecting scholarly quality, such self-defense narrows the sources of inquiry, weakens the revisability of knowledge, and damages academia’s own credibility and adaptive capacity. Generative AI lowers the costs of reading, translation, literature organization, drafting, and hypothesis formation, thereby widening the entry points into academic knowledge production. This development brings real risks, including the proliferation of low-quality discourse. But it also reveals that knowledge cannot be enclosed within institutional boundaries alone. What is needed, therefore, is neither the uncritical acceptance of AI-generated work nor its exclusion in advance, but a redesign of academic evaluation around the quality of questions, verifiability of evidence, conceptual structure, openness to criticism, and responsibility for judgment. The question posed by AI is not only whether machines can think. It is whether academia, when criticizing AI, can also criticize its own inner motives. In an age of unstoppable technological innovation, what is being tested is not only the intelligence of AI, but the metacognitive capacity of academia itself.
