# India's AI Policy Vacuum: Between Digital Public Infrastructure Ambition and Governance Readiness

**著者**: Ali Sadhik Shaik
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 法学

## Abstract

India occupies a paradoxical position in the global artificial intelligence landscape: it is simultaneously the world’s largest source of AI engineering talent (producing approximately 30–35% of the global supply of ML engineers and data scientists), a massive domestic market for AI deployment (1.4 billion potential users across a rapidly digitizing economy), an ambitious builder of population-scale digital public infrastructure (Aadhaar’s 1.3 billion identities, UPI’s 10+ billion monthly transactions, ONDC’s open commerce network), and a significant laggard in AI-specific governance. The IndiaAI Mission commits substantial public investment to sovereign compute infrastructure, indigenous foundation models, and AI skilling - yet India has no binding AI regulation, no sector-specific AI governance standards, and a Digital Personal Data Protection Act (2023) that addresses data privacy without specifically governing the AI systems that process that data. This paper examines this governance vacuum through three analytical lenses: the deployment gap (AI systems operating at population scale without regulatory guardrails in lending, hiring, healthcare, and law enforcement), the institutional gap (absence of the specialized agencies, technical expertise, and enforcement mechanisms required to implement AI governance), and the strategic tension (political incentives to delay regulation that might slow AI adoption, even as governance failures accumulate at scale). The paper introduces the India AI Governance Maturity Model (IAGMM) - a three-phase governance roadmap that sequences institutional capacity building (2026–2028), sector-specific standards (2028–2030), and cross-cutting legislation (2030–2032) in a way that supports rather than constrains India’s AI ambitions. The paper argues that India’s governance vacuum is not merely a regulatory delay but a strategic risk that threatens the sustainability, credibility, and international legitimacy of India’s AI growth trajectory. The paper concludes with implications for Indian policymakers, industry bodies (NASSCOM, iSPIRT), global enterprises deploying AI in India, development organizations, and comparative governance researchers.
