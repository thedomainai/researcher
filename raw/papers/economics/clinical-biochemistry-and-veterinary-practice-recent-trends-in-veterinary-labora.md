# Clinical Biochemistry and Veterinary Practice: Recent Trends in Veterinary Laboratory Diagnosis

**著者**: Subhasis Batabyal, Mohammed Shaz Murtuza, Ankan De
**年**: 2026 | **被引用数**: 0
**タイプ**: primary | **分野**: 経済学

## Abstract

Abstract Clinical biochemistry plays a pivotal role in modern veterinary practice, acting as a critical junction between physical examination and definitive clinical diagnosis. It is an essential component of modern veterinary laboratory diagnosis. By analyzing biofluids—primarily serum, plasma, urine, and cerebrospinal fluids, veterinarians can monitor disease progression, assess organ function, detect metabolic imbalances, and formulate targeted therapeutic plans. In last few years, the approach of veterinary laboratory diagnosis has transformed dramatically which is presently driven by technological innovations to ensure instantaneous results for precision medicine. Traditionally, clinical biochemistry and laboratory diagnosis have relied on routine measurements of metabolites, proteins, enzymes, electrolytes, and hormones. However, advanced analytical instrumentation, application of molecular biology & bioinformatics, immunodiagnostics, biosensors, point-of-care testing, and computational biology have immensely transforming the field. These developments are utmost important as because challenges such as species variation, limited reference intervals, pre-analytical errors, assay standardization, small validation cohorts, cost, and inadequate external validation remain important which can complicate interpretation. Modern clinical biochemistry now-a-days targets not only to identify disease but also to detect disease at an earlier stage, estimate prognosis, monitor therapeutic response, and support individualized treatment. Current trends in clinical biochemistry and laboratory diagnosis include automated and high-throughput chemistry platforms, point-of-care testing, improved quality assurance, disease-specific biomarkers, proteomics, metabolomics, lipidomics, liquid biopsy, multi-omics, artificial intelligence-assisted interpretation, and integration of laboratory data with precision medicine. Introduction Diagnosis is how a clinician works out what is wrong with an animal and why, and getting it right shapes everything that follows: the treatment chosen, the prognosis given, the animal's welfare, and, for production species, the owner's bottom line (Chauhan & Singh, 2024). A correct, timely diagnosis in animal health care spares animals from prolonged suffering, stops infectious disease spreading through a herd, flock, or household, and supports the surveillance systems that protect national livestock economies and, through zoonotic pathogens, human health too (Moore et al., 2007). When a diagnosis is missed or delayed in a dairy herd or poultry flock, the consequences can cascade into production losses and trade restrictions; outbreaks of foot-and-mouth disease and avian influenza show just how directly animal-disease surveillance feeds into livestock economics and, for zoonotic agents, human health risk (Moore et al., 2007). Diagnosis, in short, is not a side activity in veterinary medicine — it is the decision point the whole profession is organised around (Chauhan & Singh, 2024). Veterinarians typically group diagnosis into several complementary types (Chauhan & Singh, 2024). Clinical diagnosis draws on history-taking, observation, and physical examination to build an initial list of possibilities. Laboratory diagnosis then confirms or narrows that list by analysing blood, urine, faeces, milk, or other specimens, and covers several sub-disciplines in its own right: haematology, clinical biochemistry, microbiology and parasitology, immunodiagnosis or serology, and, increasingly, molecular diagnostics (Naveed et al., 2026; Chauhan & Singh, 2024). Diagnostic imaging — radiography, ultrasonography, and, where available, computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging — shows structural change without needing to open the animal up. Pathological diagnosis, which includes cytology, histopathology, and post-mortem examination, remains the gold standard for characterising disease at the tissue level, and other diagnostic methods are usually validated against it (Chauhan & Singh, 2024). Older approaches such as pathogen isolation, seroconversion testing, and enzyme immunoassay are increasingly being paired with faster molecular and biosensor-based methods, part of a broader shift across every diagnostic category toward testing that is quicker and less invasive (Antunes, 2026; Chauhan & Singh, 2024). None of these categories competes with the others; a vet combines them to reach a confident diagnosis. Of all these layers, laboratory diagnosis, and biochemical diagnosis in particular, is usually the fastest and most accessible once a sample has been collected (Kaneko et al., 2008; Kaneko, 1996). Laboratory diagnosis, defined simply, is the scientific process of confirming, ruling out, or characterising disease by analysing biological samples with validated methods and interpreting the results against known reference standards. Within that framework, clinical biochemistry looks specifically at blood plasma or serum, along with other body fluids such as urine, cerebrospinal fluid, and effusions, measuring substrates, enzymes, electrolytes, and hormones whose levels shift in predictable ways as disease develops (Kaneko et al., 2008). Because almost every disease process, infectious, metabolic, toxic, cancerous, or degenerative, eventually disrupts a metabolic pathway, damages a tissue, or triggers an inflammatory or hormonal response, biochemical testing gives an unusually wide first look at organ function and overall health. Raised liver enzymes point to liver injury, azotaemia points to kidney trouble, and unusual electrolyte or acid-base patterns point to specific endocrine, renal, or gastrointestinal problems, often before an animal shows obvious symptoms. That diagnostic reach, together with the relative speed, low cost, and wide availability of biochemical tests compared with imaging or histopathology, is why clinical biochemistry has remained the backbone of veterinary laboratory diagnosis for more than fifty years (Kaneko et al., 2008; Kaneko, 1996; Moore et al., 2007). That foundation depends on a disciplined workflow before, during, and after the analysis itself. Blood has to be drawn correctly, from the jugular vein in cattle, buffalo, horses, sheep, and goats; the radial or recurrent tarsal vein in dogs and cats; the ear vein in pigs; or the wing or heart vein in fowl, using handling and anticoagulants suited to both the species and the intended test: EDTA or heparin for haematology, and oxalate, citrate, or sodium fluoride for particular biochemical assays. Keeping the sample at the right temperature during transport preserves its integrity, and this pre-analytical care remains the single biggest factor in whether a result can be trusted, whatever platform eventually runs the test. Once samples reach the laboratory, clinicians usually order grouped panels built around a suspected organ system, renal, hepatic, diabetic, electrolyte, pancreatic, thyroid, lipid, or cardiac, rather than requesting analytes one at a time. Serum enzyme activity remains one of the most sensitive markers of tissue injury: AST and ALT flag liver and muscle damage, LDH flags heart and liver damage, ALP flags liver and bone disease, and CK flags heart and skeletal-muscle injury. Urinalysis complements these serum panels cheaply, combining a physical check of colour, odour, and specific gravity with chemical screening for pH, protein, glucose, ketones, blood, and bile pigments; simple bench tests such as Rothera's test for ketones or Benedict's test for glucose remain the fastest screening options at first-opinion practice level. None of these results mean much without the right reference interval to compare them against, and choosing that interval carefully, accounting for age, sex, breed, species, and increasingly breed-specific variation within a single species, matters just as much as the measurement itself (Chang et al., 2016). The history of veterinary laboratory diagnosis traces a clear line from descriptive pathology to quantitative, multi-analyte biochemistry and, more recently, to molecular and computational medicine. Early veterinary diagnosis relied almost entirely on post-mortem anatomical and histological description, cataloguing lesions and symptoms in the tradition of comparative pathology. By the mid-twentieth century, quantitative clinical chemistry had joined this descriptive approach, as spectrophotometric and colourimetric assays made it possible to measure specific analytes in blood and urine instead of relying only on visible or microscopic lesions. The discipline also became formally organised during this period: veterinary laboratory diagnosticians in North America began meeting annually in the 1950s in response to a fast-changing livestock and poultry industry and parallel advances in diagnostic technology, and in 1968 they formally founded the American Association of Veterinary Laboratory Diagnosticians, tasked with setting quality guidelines for diagnostic laboratories alongside sharing diagnostic knowledge (Chauhan & Singh, 2024). Dedicated societies and journals followed, including the American and European Societies of Veterinary Clinical Pathology and the Journal of Veterinary Diagnostic Investigation, both still central to advances in the field (Chauhan & Singh, 2024). From the 1970s, automated analysers extended how much biochemical testing laboratories could handle and how broadly; from the 1990s, PCR and related molecular techniques were gradually adopted, shifting pathogen detection away from culture and serology and toward direct nucleic-acid detection (Naveed et al., 2026).
