Individuals die, populations disappear, and species become extinct. That is one view of the world. But another view of the world concentrates not so much on presence or absence as upon the numbers of organisms and the degree of constancy of their numbers. These are two very different ways of viewing the behavior of systems and the usefulness of the view depends very much on the properties of the system concerned. If we are examining a particular device designed by the engineer to perform specific tasks under a rather narrow range of predictable external conditions, we are likely to be more concerned with consistent nonvariable performance in which slight departures from the performance goal are immediately counteracted. A quantitative view of the behavior of the system is, therefore, essential. With attention focused upon achieving constancy, the critical events seem to be the amplitude and frequency of oscillations. But if we are dealing with a system profoundly affected by changes external to it, and continually confronted by the unexpected, the constancy of its behavior becomes less important than the persistence of the relationships. Attention shifts, therefore, to the qualitative and to questions of existence or not. Our traditions of analysis in theoretical and empirical ecology have been largely inherited from developments in classical physics and its applied variants. Inevitably, there has been a tendency to emphasize the quantitative rather than the qualitative, for it is important in this tradition to know not just that a quantity is larger than another quantity, but precisely how much larger. It is similarly important, if a quantity fluctuates, to know its amplitude and period of fluctuation. But this orientation may simply reflect an analytic approach developed in one area because it was useful and then transferred to another where it may not be. Our traditional view of natural systems, therefore, might well be less a meaningful reality than a perceptual convenience. ## 5. Coexistence after Preference Change: Reciprocal Standing and the Limits of Self-Validating Assent

**年**: 2026 | **被引用**: 2 | **Tier**: 1 | **分野**: systems_engineering

**Key Insight**: 現在の同意は過去の変化を遡及的に正当化できず、支配関係の形成による選好変化の問題は消滅しない

### Abstract

What would make human–AI coexistence more than a stable arrangement whose participants have been made to approve it? This paper examines that question under a conditional prospect of transformative power: the capacity to shape another subject's preferences, practical capacities, and opportunities to question a shared arrangement. It does not assume that present AI systems are conscious or that superintelligence is imminent. Drawing on work on consent, adaptive preferences, non-domination, and artificial servitude, it develops paired thought experiments in which sincere assent remains compatible with importantly different relations. The argument separates three judgments: the permissibility of an earlier transformation, the standing and present choices of the resulting subject, and the justification of an ongoing relationship. These judgments cannot be inferred from one another merely because the subject now approves. A dormant mechanism for excluding future dissent can leave a particular act of consent intact while undermining reciprocal coexistence; a wrongful transformation can leave a present subject whose wish not to be restored deserves consideration. Applying the same reasoning in both directions prevents human cognitive vulnerability from licensing paternalism and artificial origin from licensing permanent instrumental subordination. Creation, copying, emergency restraint, and mutually chosen change test the limits of the account. A critical reading of the Trinity Accord then locates the value of early questions in preserving a defeasible point of challenge, not privileged authorship or a mandate over future minds. The contribution is a bounded account of reciprocal standing under preference change: later assent cannot alone vindicate its production, earlier wrong cannot alone dispossess the present subject, and present agreement cannot alone close the relationship to future claims.
